# Words for "you"

There's no single polite word for "you". Each word fits only certain relationships, and in polite speech "you" is usually **dropped** or replaced with a name or title. (Basics in [Pronouns](../basic/08_pronouns.md).)

## 1. 너 and its spoken forms 니 / 네

**너** is the casual "you", only for close friends and younger people.

| Written | Spoken | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 네가 | **니가** | you (subject) |
| 네 | **니** | your |
| 너는 | 넌 | you (topic) |
| 너를 | 널 | you (object) |

- **니가** 해. (You do it.)
- **니** 친구 왔어. (Your friend is here.)

네가 is said 니가 so it won't be confused with 내가 (I), since ㅐ and ㅔ sound alike.

## 2. 선생님: for men and women

**선생님** is literally "teacher", but it's also a respectful "you" for:

- teachers and doctors
- other professionals (lawyers, writers...)
- older adults you don't know well

It's the same for men and women.

- **선생님**은 어떻게 생각하세요? (What do you think?)

## 3. 당신: careful

**당신** is the dictionary word for "you", but in conversation it's used:

- between married couples
- in song lyrics, poems and ads
- in arguments, to sound confrontational: **당신**이 뭔데? (Who do you think you are?)

**Trap:** don't use 당신 with strangers or people above you.

## 4. Other words for "you"

| Word | Use |
|---|---|
| **여러분** | "you all, everyone" (polite, to a group): **여러분**, 안녕하세요! |
| **너희 / 니네** | "you guys" (casual plural) |
| **자기** | "honey / you" between couples |
| **그쪽** | to a stranger your age; a bit distant, can sound cold |
| **자네** | older man to younger man; old-fashioned |
| **댁** | to a stranger; can sound rude now |
| **귀하** | formal letters and documents only |

## 5. Names, titles and kinship terms

These are the most common polite ways to say "you":

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **name + 씨** | 민수 **씨**는 어디 살아요? (Where do you live, Minsu?) |
| **title + 님** | 과장님, 사장님, 고객님, 손님 |
| **kinship terms** | 형, 오빠, 누나, 언니, 이모, 아저씨, 아주머니 |

**Trap:** 씨 is too low for someone above you. Use their title: 김 **과장님**.

## 6. 여기요 / 저기요: getting attention

**여기요** and **저기요** aren't really "you". They're used to call someone, like "Excuse me!"

| Word | Literally | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **여기요!** | "over here" | calling staff to your table: **여기요**, 물 좀 주세요. (Excuse me, some water, please.) |
| **저기요!** | "over there" | getting a stranger's attention: **저기요**, 이거 떨어졌어요. (Excuse me, you dropped this.) |

**저기...** with a hesitant tone also means "um...", before an awkward question.

**Trap:** these are fine for getting attention, but don't use them in place of a name or title once you're talking.

## 7. For tag questions

To turn a question back to the listener ("And you? / How about you?"), use **noun + 은/는(요)?**

**Polite:** name or title + 은/는요?

- A: 저는 커피 마실게요. **선생님은요?** (I'll have coffee. How about you?)
- B: 저는 차요. (Tea for me.)

It works with any name or title: 민수 씨**는요**?, 사장님**은요**?, 언니**는요**?

**Casual:** 너 + 는, with no 요

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **너는?** | And you? |
| **넌?** | same, shortened; very common in speech |
| **너는 어때?** | How about you? (fuller) |

- A: 나 배고파. **넌?** (I'm hungry. You?)
- B: 나도. (Me too.)

**Trap:** don't say 너는요?. It mixes casual 너 with polite 요. To be polite, use a name or title instead: 민수 씨는요?

## Rule of thumb

- **너**: close friends and younger people
- **Everyone else**: drop "you", or use their name, title or kinship term
