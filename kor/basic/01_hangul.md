# Hangul (한글)

Korean is written in **한글 (Hangul)**, an alphabet designed on purpose by King Sejong and his scholars in 1443 and published in 1446 as **훈민정음** ("the correct sounds for teaching the people"). Before that, Koreans wrote in Chinese characters. Hangul was made so ordinary people could read and write. (Hangul Day, 한글날, is October 9.)

Hangul is a real alphabet (each letter is one sound), but letters are **grouped into syllable blocks** instead of written in a line: ㅎ + ㅏ + ㄴ → **한**, ㄱ + ㅡ + ㄹ → **글**.

Modern Hangul has **24 basic letters**: 14 consonants and 10 vowels. Doubling and combining them gives **40** in total: 19 consonants and 21 vowels.

### Why the shapes look the way they do

The basic consonants are **pictures of the mouth** making the sound:

| Base | Picture of... |
|---|---|
| ㄱ | back of the tongue touching the soft palate |
| ㄴ | tongue tip touching the ridge behind the teeth |
| ㅁ | the closed mouth (lips) |
| ㅅ | a tooth |
| ㅇ | the open throat |

Other letters **add a stroke** to show a stronger (aspirated) sound: ㄱ → **ㅋ**, ㄴ → ㄷ → **ㅌ**, ㅁ → ㅂ → **ㅍ**, ㅅ → ㅈ → **ㅊ**, ㅇ → **ㅎ**. So letters that look alike sound alike.

## Vowels (모음)

The vowels are built from three symbols: **·** (heaven), **ㅡ** (earth), **ㅣ** (human). A second short stroke adds a **y-** sound in front: ㅏ a → **ㅑ ya**.

## Building syllable blocks

Every block is **one syllable** and always has the order **consonant → vowel → (optional) final consonant**.

**1. A block must start with a consonant.** If the syllable begins with a vowel sound, the silent **ㅇ** fills the consonant slot: 아, 오, 이. (At the *end* of a block, ㅇ is pronounced "ng": 강 *gang* (river).)

**2. Where the vowel goes depends on its shape:**

| Vowel type | Position | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Vertical (ㅏ ㅑ ㅓ ㅕ ㅣ ㅐ ㅔ…) | to the **right** of the consonant | 가, 너 (you), 비 (rain), 새 (bird) |
| Horizontal (ㅗ ㅛ ㅜ ㅠ ㅡ) | **below** the consonant | 고, 누 (trouble), 므 |
| Combined (ㅘ ㅝ ㅢ…) | below **and** to the right | 과 (lesson), 뭐 (what), 의 ('s) |

**3. A final consonant (받침, "support")** goes at the bottom: 가 + ㄱ → **각** (angle), 사 + ㄹ → **살** (flesh), 모 + ㅁ → **몸** (body).

Some blocks have a **double final** (겹받침), made of two different consonants: **ㄳ ㄵ ㄶ ㄺ ㄻ ㄼ ㄽ ㄾ ㄿ ㅀ ㅄ**, as in 닭 (chicken), 값 (price), 앉다 (to sit). Only one of the two is pronounced at the end of a word (see [Pronunciation](./02_pronunciation.md#final-consonants-받침)).

> 한국어 (Korean language) → 한 (ㅎ+ㅏ+ㄴ) · 국 (ㄱ+ㅜ+ㄱ) · 어 (ㅇ+ㅓ)

Whether a word ends in a **받침** matters in grammar: many particles and endings have two forms depending on it (책**을** (book) vs 사과**를** (apple); see [Word classes](./03_word_classes.md#조사-particle)).

## Dictionary order (가나다 order)

Korean dictionaries sort by consonant first, then vowel, then 받침. Double consonants come right after their single letter.

- **Consonants:** ㄱ ㄲ ㄴ ㄷ ㄸ ㄹ ㅁ ㅂ ㅃ ㅅ ㅆ ㅇ ㅈ ㅉ ㅊ ㅋ ㅌ ㅍ ㅎ
- **Vowels:** ㅏ ㅐ ㅑ ㅒ ㅓ ㅔ ㅕ ㅖ ㅗ ㅘ ㅙ ㅚ ㅛ ㅜ ㅝ ㅞ ㅟ ㅠ ㅡ ㅢ ㅣ

## Spacing (띄어쓰기)

Korean puts spaces between words, like English. The catch is that **particles attach to the word before them with no space**, while most other words are separated:

- 저**는** 학교**에** 가요. ✓ (I go to school.)
- 저 는 학교 에 가요. ✗

Punctuation (. , ? !) works like English.

## Chinese characters in Korean (한자)

### Three layers of vocabulary

Korean words come from three sources:

| Layer | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **고유어** (native words) | original Korean words | 하나 (one), 사람 (person), 먹다 (to eat), 예쁘다 (to be pretty) |
| **한자어** (Sino-Korean words) | built from Chinese characters, pronounced the Korean way | 학생 (student), 학교 (school), 가족 (family), 음식 (food) |
| **외래어** (loanwords) | mostly from English | 커피 (coffee), 컴퓨터 (computer), 버스 (bus) |

**한자어 make up roughly half to 60% of the dictionary**, and even more of formal, academic, legal, and news vocabulary. Everyday basics tend to be native words.

### Where you actually see 한자 (hanja) today

In modern South Korea, hanja are **rarely written** in everyday text, and almost everything is in Hangul. You'll still meet them in:

- **Newspaper headlines**, as one-character abbreviations: 美 (the US), 中 (China), 日 (Japan), 韓 (Korea)
- **Personal names**: almost every Korean name has hanja behind it, registered officially, even though it's written in Hangul
- **Law, academic writing, older books**, and dictionaries (to show a word's origin)
- **Signs and traditional settings**: shop signs, temples, ancestral rites

South Korean middle and high schools teach a set of **1,800 basic hanja** (교육용 기초 한자). North Korea stopped using hanja in everyday writing.

### How a hanja is read

Each character has (usually) **one Korean reading, always one syllable**. Koreans name a character by giving its **meaning + its sound** (훈음):

| Hanja | Called | Meaning | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|
| 學 | 배울 학 | learn | 학 |
| 生 | 날 생 | be born, live | 생 |
| 校 | 학교 교 | school | 교 |
| 大 | 큰 대 | big | 대 |
| 人 | 사람 인 | person | 인 |
| 國 | 나라 국 | country | 국 |
| 韓 | 한국 한 | Korea | 한 |
| 外 | 바깥 외 | outside | 외 |

### Why it's worth knowing: roots

You don't need to *write* hanja, but knowing the **meaning behind a syllable** unlocks vocabulary. It works like Latin/Greek roots in English (*bio-*, *-logy*).

| Root | Words |
|---|---|
| 학 (學, learn) | **학**생 (student), **학**교 (school), 대**학** (university), 과**학** (science) |
| 생 (生, life/born) | 학**생** (student), **생**일 (birthday, "born-day"), 선**생**님 (teacher) |
| 국 (國, country) | 한**국** (Korea), 중**국** (China), 외**국** (foreign country), **국**가 (nation) |
| 인 (人, person) | 한국**인** (Korean person), 외국**인** (foreigner), **인**구 (population) |

So 외국인 = 外 (outside) + 國 (country) + 人 (person) = "foreigner".

### Homonyms

Because many different hanja share the same Korean sound, Sino-Korean has lots of **homonyms**. Hangul alone doesn't tell them apart; context does.

- **수**: 水 (water), 手 (hand), 數 (number)
- **전**: 前 (before), 電 (electric), 全 (whole)
- **정**: 情 (affection), 正 (correct), 定 (fix)

### Native vs Sino-Korean pairs

Many ideas have both a native word and a 한자어. The Sino-Korean one is usually **more formal**, **technical**, or **honorific**:

| Native | Sino-Korean | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 나이 | 연세 (年歲) | age (연세 is honorific) |
| 집 | 댁 (宅) | house (댁 is honorific) |
| 이 | 치아 (齒牙) | teeth (치아 is more formal) |
| 하나, 둘, 셋 | 일, 이, 삼 | numbers (two parallel systems; see [Word classes](./03_word_classes.md#수사-numeral)) |
