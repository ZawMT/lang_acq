# Nine Word Classes (9품사)

Korean school grammar sorts every word into **nine 품사 (parts of speech)**. They're grouped by the **job** a word does in a sentence. Korean is built by attaching things to the end of words, and the grouping reflects that.

| Group | Job | Classes |
|---|---|---|
| **체언** (substantives) | the "things" in a sentence; take particles | 명사, 대명사, 수사 |
| **관계언** (relational words) | glue a word to its role in the sentence | 조사 |
| **용언** (predicates) | describe actions/states; conjugate | 동사, 형용사 |
| **수식언** (modifiers) | modify another word; never change | 관형사, 부사 |
| **독립언** (independent words) | stand apart from the sentence | 감탄사 |

Only **용언** (and the particle 이다) change form (**가변어**, variable). Everything else is **불변어** (invariable), which is the reverse of French, where most classes inflect.

## 체언 — Substantives

### 명사 (noun)

Names a person, place, thing, or idea. No gender, no articles, and plural marking (**-들**) is optional. Usually it's left off when context makes it clear.

- Example: **사람** (person), **학교** (school), **사랑** (love), **서울** (Seoul)
- Sentence: **학생**이 **학교**에 가요. (The student goes to school.)

A special sub-type, **의존 명사** (dependent nouns), can't stand alone and always needs a modifier in front: **것** (thing), **수** (way/possibility), **데** (place), **줄**. They power many grammar patterns: 먹을 **수** 있어요 (I can eat).

### 대명사 (pronoun)

Replaces a noun. Korean pronouns change with **politeness**, not with case. 나 vs 저 are both "I", and the choice depends on who you're talking to.

- Example: **나/저** (I: casual/humble), **너** (you, casual), **우리/저희** (we: casual/humble), **이것** (this thing), **거기** (there), **누구** (who), **무엇** (what)
- Sentence: **저**는 학생이에요. (I am a student.)

Korean often drops pronouns entirely, and "you" is especially avoided in polite speech (see [Sentence structure](./04_sentence_structure.md)).

### 수사 (numeral)

Numbers get their own class. There are **two parallel number systems**: native Korean and Sino-Korean.

- Example: **하나, 둘, 셋** (one, two, three: native) / **일, 이, 삼** (one, two, three: Sino-Korean) / **첫째, 둘째** (first, second)
- Sentence: 사과 **하나**를 주세요. (Please give me one apple.)

**Trap:** when a number sits directly in front of a noun or counter, it's classed as a **관형사** (below), not a 수사, and the native forms shorten: 하나 → **한**, 둘 → **두**, 셋 → **세**, 넷 → **네**. So it's **한** 사람 (one person), not "하나 사람".

## 관계언 — Relational words

### 조사 (particle)

Particles are the class that most sets Korean apart. A particle attaches **directly to the end** of a noun (with no space) and marks its role: subject, object, topic, place, "also", "only", and so on. They do the job of English word order and prepositions. That's why Korean word order can be flexible: the particles already say who did what.

- Example: **이/가** (subject), **은/는** (topic), **을/를** (object), **에** (at/to), **에서** (at/from), **의** ('s), **도** (also), **만** (only)
- Sentence: 저**는** 사과**를** 먹어요. (I eat an apple.)

Most particles come in pairs that depend on whether the noun ends in a consonant (받침) or a vowel: 책**을** (book) / 사과**를** (apple). Full breakdown in [Particles](./05_particles.md).

**Oddity:** **이다** ("to be", as in "X is Y") is officially a particle (**서술격 조사**), not a verb, because it glues onto a noun (학생**이에요**, "(I) am a student"). Unlike every other particle, it conjugates.

## 용언 — Predicates (conjugate)

Both classes have a **stem** and a dictionary form ending in **-다**: 먹**다** (to eat) → stem 먹-. All grammar is added by attaching **endings (어미)** to the stem.

### 동사 (verb)

Shows an action or process.

- Example: **가다** (to go), **먹다** (to eat), **하다** (to do), **공부하다** (to study)
- Sentence: 친구가 밥을 **먹어요**. (My friend is eating.)

### 형용사 (adjective / descriptive verb)

Describes a state or quality, and **conjugates just like a verb**. A Korean adjective is a full predicate on its own, with no "to be" needed. 추워요 means "(it) is cold" by itself. That's why many textbooks call them **descriptive verbs**.

- Example: **크다** (to be big), **예쁘다** (to be pretty), **좋다** (to be good), **춥다** (to be cold), **있다/없다** (to exist / not exist)
- Sentence: 날씨가 **추워요**. (The weather is cold.)

How 동사 and 형용사 still differ:

| | 동사 (먹다, to eat) | 형용사 (예쁘다, to be pretty) |
|---|---|---|
| Plain present | 먹**는다** | 예쁘**다** (no -는다) |
| Before a noun (now) | 먹**는** 사람 (a person who eats) | 예쁜 꽃 (a pretty flower) |
| Command / "let's" | 먹어라, 먹자 ✓ | ✗ (can't command "be pretty") |
| Progressive -고 있다 | 먹고 있다 ✓ | ✗ |

## 수식언 — Modifiers (never change)

### 관형사 (determiner / pre-noun modifier)

Sits in front of a noun and modifies it. It **never conjugates and never takes a particle**. This is a small, closed class.

- Example: **이, 그, 저** (this / that / that over there), **새** (new), **헌** (old, worn), **모든** (all), **무슨** (what kind of), **어느** (which), **한/두/세** (one / two / three)
- Sentence: **새** 차를 샀어요. (I bought a new car.)

**Trap:** most English adjectives before a noun ("a pretty flower") are **not** 관형사 in Korean. They're 형용사 with a modifier ending attached: 예쁘다 (to be pretty) → 예쁜 꽃 (a pretty flower). Only a handful of words are true 관형사.

### 부사 (adverb)

Modifies a verb, adjective, another adverb, or a whole sentence.

- Example: **빨리** (quickly), **아주** (very), **잘** (well), **안** (not), **못** (cannot), **벌써** (already), **그리고** (and then)
- Sentence: 한국어를 **잘** 못해요. (I'm not good at Korean.)

Korean negation lives here: **안** (not) and **못** (cannot) are adverbs placed before the verb.

## 독립언 — Independent words

### 감탄사 (interjection)

Expresses feeling, calls out, or answers. It stands apart from the rest of the sentence. **네/예** (yes) and **아니요** (no) belong here.

- Example: **아** (ah), **와** (wow), **어머** (oh my!), **아이고** (oh dear / ugh), **네** (yes), **아니요** (no), **여보세요** (hello, on the phone)
- Sentence: **와**, 정말 맛있어요! (Wow, it's really tasty!)

## Where did the conjunction go?

There's no conjunction class. English "and / but / so" is split across **three different tools**, depending on what's being joined:

| Joining... | Tool | "and" | "but" | "so" |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| two nouns | 조사 (particle) | 와/과, 하고, (이)랑 | — | — |
| two sentences | 부사 (conjunctive adverb) | 그리고 | 그런데, 하지만 | 그래서 |
| two clauses in one sentence | 어미 (connective ending) | -고 | -지만 | -아서/어서 |

- 빵**하고** 우유 (bread and milk)
- 비가 와요. **그래서** 집에 있어요. (It's raining. So I'm staying home.)
- 비가 와**서** 집에 있어요. (Because it's raining, I'm staying home.)

## Not a class, but everywhere: 어미 (endings)

Endings aren't one of the 9 품사, because they aren't words. They're pieces attached to a predicate's stem, and they carry most of the grammar that English expresses with separate words:

| English uses... | Korean uses an ending |
|---|---|
| auxiliaries (did, will) | -었-, -겠- |
| politeness (words like "sir", tone) | -요, -습니다, honorific -시- |
| conjunctions (and, but, because) | -고, -지만, -아서 |
| relative clauses (who, that) | -는, -(으)ㄴ, -(으)ㄹ |
| sentence type (statement / question / command) | -다 / -까 / -아라 |

They stack in a fixed order onto the stem:

**가** (stem of 가다, to go) + **시** (honorific) + **었** (past) + **어요** (polite ending) → **가셨어요** ((someone respected) went)

Covered properly in [Verb endings](./06_verb_endings.md).

## English 8 → Korean 9 at a glance

| English | Korean |
|---|---|
| Noun | 명사 (+ 수사 for numbers) |
| Pronoun | 대명사 |
| Verb | 동사 |
| Adjective | 형용사 (as predicate) / 관형사 (fixed pre-noun words) |
| Adverb | 부사 |
| Preposition | 조사 (and they come *after* the noun: **post**positions) |
| Conjunction | no class: 조사 / 부사 / 어미 |
| Interjection | 감탄사 |
| *(no equivalent)* | 어미 (endings), not a word class but the core of Korean grammar |
