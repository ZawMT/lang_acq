# Sentence Structure (문장 구조)

 In Korean, words combine into phrases, phrases into clauses, clauses into a sentence. Korean puts the **predicate (verb or adjective) last**, and particles on the end of each noun tell you what role it plays.

- Basic order: **Subject – Object – Verb**
- Sentence: 저는 사과를 **먹어요**. (I eat an apple.)

Grammar terms you'll meet in Korean textbooks:

| Term | Korean | Careful |
|---|---|---|
| Word | **단어** | Particles count as separate 단어 in school grammar, even though they're written attached |
| "Spacing unit" | **어절** | A word + its particles/endings, written between spaces |
| Phrase | **구** | 명사구 (noun phrase), 동사구 (verb phrase)... |
| Clause | **절** | |
| Sentence | **문장** | |
| Subject / Predicate | **주어 / 서술어** | 서술어 is the verb, adjective, or noun + 이다 |

## 단어 and 어절 (word and spacing unit)

A **단어** is the smallest unit with a word class (see [the nine word classes](./03_word_classes.md)). An **어절** is what sits between two spaces: a content word plus whatever particles or endings are glued onto it.

- Sentence: **저는 / 학생이에요.** (I am a student.)
  - 2 어절: 저는, 학생이에요
  - 4 단어: 저 (pronoun) + 는 (particle) + 학생 (noun) + 이에요 (particle 이다, conjugated)

This is why Korean spacing (see [Spacing](./01_hangul.md)) puts a space **after** the particle, never before it: 저는 학생이에요, not "저 는 학생 이에요".

## 구 (phrase)

Two or more 어절 acting as one unit, **without** its own subject + predicate pair.

| Type | Example | Acts like a |
|---|---|---|
| 명사구 (noun phrase) | **새 차** (a new car) | noun |
| 동사구 (verb phrase) | **밥을 먹다** (to eat a meal) | verb |
| 형용사구 (adjective phrase) | **아주 예쁘다** (to be very pretty) | adjective |
| 부사구 (adverb phrase) | **아주 빨리** (very quickly) | adverb |

- Sentence: **우리 형이** **아주 빨리** 달려요. (My older brother runs very fast.)

## 절 (clause)

A unit with its own **주어 + 서술어** that sits inside a bigger sentence. The subject is often dropped, but it's still understood.

- 비가 와서 (because it's raining): 주어 비가, 서술어 와서
- 내가 산 (that I bought): 주어 내가, 서술어 산

A clause can't end the sentence on its own because it doesn't carry a **final ending**. Its ending (-아서, -는, -고...) marks it as dependent. Only the last predicate takes a sentence-ending form like -아요/-어요.

## Sentence components (문장 성분)

Korean school grammar labels every part of a sentence by its **job**. Particles usually tell you which job:

| Component | Job | Usual marker | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **주어** (subject) | who/what does or is | 이/가, 은/는, 께서 | **민수가** 왔어요. (Minsu came.) |
| **서술어** (predicate) | the action, state, or "is" | conjugated ending | 민수가 **왔어요**. (Minsu came.) |
| **목적어** (object) | what the action is done to | 을/를 | 저는 **커피를** 마셔요. (I drink coffee.) |
| **보어** (complement) | what something becomes / is not | 이/가 + 되다 or 아니다 | 물이 **얼음이** 됐어요. (The water became ice.) |
| **관형어** (noun modifier) | describes a noun | 관형사, -(으)ㄴ/-는, 의 | **새** 차 (a new car), **예쁜** 꽃 (a pretty flower), **친구의** 책 (a friend's book) |
| **부사어** (adverbial) | how/when/where/why | 부사, 에, 에서, (으)로... | **빨리** 가요. (Go quickly.) **학교에** 가요. (I go to school.) |
| **독립어** (independent) | stands outside the sentence | 감탄사, name + 아/야 | **네**, 알겠어요. (Yes, I understand.) **민수야**, 가자! (Minsu, let's go!) |

**Trap:** 보어 is a narrow category. In Korean school grammar it's **only** the noun + 이/가 before **되다** (to become) and **아니다** (to not be):

- 저는 **의사가** 되고 싶어요. (I want to become a doctor.)
- 저는 **학생이** 아니에요. (I'm not a student.)

In 저는 학생이에요 (I'm a student), 학생이에요 as a whole is the 서술어, not a 보어.

## Word order

### The predicate goes last

This is the one rule that's (almost) never broken in written Korean. Everything else can move; the predicate stays at the end.

- 저는 어제 도서관에서 친구하고 한국어를 **공부했어요**. (I studied Korean with a friend at the library yesterday.)

The typical order of the middle part is **topic/subject → time → place → with whom → object → predicate**, but because particles mark each role, you can shuffle them for emphasis:

- 저는 사과를 먹었어요. (I ate an apple.)
- 사과를 저는 먹었어요. (The apple, I ate.) (same meaning, focus shifts)

In casual speech people sometimes tack something on after the predicate as an afterthought: 먹었어, **사과**? (Did you eat it, the apple?). Treat this as spoken style only.

### Modifiers come first

Korean is consistently **head-last**: whatever describes something comes **before** it, and particles come **after** the noun they mark.

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| Modifying clause + noun | **한국에서 온** 친구 (a friend who came from Korea) |
| Noun + particle | 학교**에** (to school) |
| Possessor + noun | **친구의** 책 (my friend's book) |
| Subordinate + main clause | **비가 와서** 집에 있었어요 (Because it rained, I stayed home.) |
| Adverb + predicate | **빨리** 가요 (Go quickly.) |

### Questions don't change the order

A question has the **same order** as the statement; only the ending (and intonation) changes. Question words stay where the answer would go:

- 민수가 **학교에** 가요. (Minsu goes to school.)
- 민수가 **어디에** 가요? (Where does Minsu go?)

## Topic vs subject

Korean has two ways to mark "the thing the sentence is about":

- **은/는** marks the **topic** ("as for X...")
- **이/가** marks the grammatical **subject**

They can both appear in one sentence, which gives the famous "double subject" pattern:

- **코끼리는** **코가** 길어요. (Elephants have long trunks. Literally "As for elephants, the trunk is long.")

Here 코끼리는 is the topic of the whole sentence, and 코가 길어요 is itself a small clause (a **서술절**, predicate clause) with its own subject. The full 이/가 vs 은/는 story is in [Particles](./05_particles.md).

## Dropping (생략)

If the listener can work it out from context, Korean leaves it out, especially **subjects and objects**. A complete sentence can be just a predicate:

- A: 밥 먹었어요? (Did [you] eat a meal?)
- B: 네, 먹었어요. (Yes, [I] ate [it].)

A drops "you", and B drops both "I" and the object 밥. A also drops the object particle: 밥**을** → 밥, which is normal in speech. Adding them back (**저는** 밥을 먹었어요) sounds heavy or contrastive ("*I* ate, but someone else didn't").

"You" in particular is avoided in polite speech. Koreans use the person's name + 씨, a title (선생님, teacher), or just drop it (see [Pronouns](./08_pronouns.md)).

## Sentence types by purpose

Korean marks sentence type with the **final ending**, not with word order or punctuation alone. Each speech level (see [Speech levels](./07_speech_levels.md)) has its own set of endings:

| Type | Purpose | Formal (합쇼체) | Polite (해요체) | Casual (해체) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 평서문 (declarative) | statement | 갑니다 | 가요. | 가. |
| 의문문 (interrogative) | question | 갑니까? | 가요? ↗ | 가? ↗ |
| 명령문 (imperative) | command | 가십시오 | 가세요. | 가. |
| 청유문 (propositive) | "let's..." | 갑시다 | 가요. | 가자. |
| 감탄문 (exclamative) | surprise, emotion | — | 가네요! / 가는군요! | 가네! / 가는구나! |

**Trap:** in 해요체, statement, question, command, and "let's" can all use the **same form**. 가요 means "(I) go", "(do you) go?", "go!", or "let's go", and only intonation and context tell them apart. The formal level keeps them separate, which is why it's used for announcements and news.

### Affirmative and negative

Positive/negative is a **form**, not a sentence type. Korean has two ways to negate, placing 안/못 before the predicate or adding -지 않다/-지 못하다 after the stem:

- Affirmative: 저는 고기를 먹어요. (I eat meat.)
- Negative: 저는 고기를 **안** 먹어요. / 저는 고기를 먹**지 않아요**. (I don't eat meat.)

Details in [Negation](./16_negation.md).

**Trap: answering a negative question.** 네 means "that's right" and 아니요 means "that's not right", so they agree or disagree with the **question**, not with the fact:

- A: 고기 안 먹어요? (You don't eat meat?)
- B: **네**, 안 먹어요. (Right, I don't.) / **아니요**, 먹어요. (No, I do.)

English speakers say "No, I don't" here. In Korean that's **네**.

## Sentences by structure

| Type | Made of | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **홑문장** (simple) | one 주어 + 서술어 | 비가 와요. (It's raining.) |
| **이어진 문장**, 대등 (coordinate) | two equal clauses joined by an ending | 비가 오**고** 바람이 불어요. (It's raining and the wind is blowing.) |
| **이어진 문장**, 종속 (subordinate) | a dependent clause + main clause | 비가 와**서** 집에 있어요. (Because it's raining, I'm staying home.) |
| **안은 문장** (embedding) | a clause placed **inside** another as a part | [내가 어제 **산**] 책이 재미있어요. (The book I bought yesterday is interesting.) |

Clauses are joined by **connective endings** on the first predicate (-고, -지만, -아서/어서), not by separate joining words. See [Connective endings](./19_connective_endings.md).

An embedded clause (안긴 문장) can play any role in the bigger sentence:

| Embedded clause as a... | Ending | Example |
|---|---|---|
| noun (명사절) | -기, -(으)ㅁ, -는 것 | [한국어를 배우**기**]가 재미있어요. (Learning Korean is fun.) |
| modifier (관형절) | -는, -(으)ㄴ, -(으)ㄹ | [안경을 **쓴**] 사람 (the person who wears glasses) |
| adverb (부사절) | -게, -이, -도록 | 그는 [소리도 **없이**] 왔어요. (He came without a sound.) |
| predicate (서술절) | none (it's a whole clause) | 코끼리는 [코가 **길어요**]. (Elephants have long trunks.) |
| quotation (인용절) | -다고, -라고 | 민수가 [내일 **온다고**] 했어요. (Minsu said he'd come tomorrow.) |

See [Modifier clauses](./20_modifier_clauses.md) and [Reported speech](./21_reported_speech.md) for these.

## Expressions and idioms (관용 표현)

**표현** (expression) is the loose everyday term for any string of words with a meaning. **관용구** / **관용 표현** is the stricter term for a fixed expression whose meaning isn't the sum of its parts.

- Example: **발이 넓다** (literally "to have wide feet", meaning to know a lot of people)

See [Idioms](./23_idioms.md) for more.

## How they fit together

```
단어  →  어절  →  구  →  절 (주어 + 서술어)  →  문장
```

- A **단어** is the smallest piece with a word class (particles included).
- A content word plus its particles/endings makes an **어절**, the unit you put spaces around.
- **어절** group into a **구** (no subject + predicate pair of its own).
- A **주어** plus a **서술어** makes a **절**. Its ending decides whether it's a dependent clause or the end of the sentence.
- One or more **절**, with the **predicate last** and a **final ending** on it, form a **문장**.
