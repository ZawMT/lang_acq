# Pronunciation (발음)

Hangul spelling is very regular, but Korean spelling shows the **dictionary form of each piece** (the stem, the particle) rather than exactly what you say. When syllables meet, sounds change in predictable ways. So the job here is learning (1) the sounds of the letters and (2) the **sound-change rules** that apply between syllables.

In this file, **[brackets in Hangul]** show how a word is actually pronounced: 한국어 (Korean language) [한구거].

## Consonants: the three-way contrast

English separates consonants by **voicing** (b vs p, d vs t, g vs k). Korean doesn't. Instead, it separates them by **breath and tension**, giving **three versions** of each stop and of ㅈ:

| Plain (lax) | Aspirated (with a puff of air) | Tense (tight throat, no puff) |
|---|---|---|
| ㄱ | ㅋ | ㄲ |
| ㄷ | ㅌ | ㄸ |
| ㅂ | ㅍ | ㅃ |
| ㅈ | ㅊ | ㅉ |
| ㅅ | — | ㅆ |

Minimal sets to practice:

| Plain | Aspirated | Tense |
|---|---|---|
| 달 (moon) | 탈 (mask) | 딸 (daughter) |
| 불 (fire) | 풀 (grass) | 뿔 (horn) |
| 자다 (to sleep) | 차다 (to kick) | 짜다 (to be salty) |
| 사다 (to buy) | — | 싸다 (to be cheap) |

Tips:

- **Aspirated ㅋ ㅌ ㅍ ㅊ** have a strong puff, stronger than English k/t/p.
- **Tense ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅉ ㅆ** have no puff and a tight throat. They're like the "k" in *sky* or the "p" in *spy*, said sharply.

### Plain consonants change with position

The **plain ㄱ ㄷ ㅂ ㅈ** don't have one fixed sound. How they sound depends on where they sit in the word:

| Letter | Start of a word | Between vowels, or after ㄴ ㅁ ㅇ ㄹ | After a [ㄱ ㄷ ㅂ] 받침 |
|---|---|---|---|
| ㄱ | soft "k": **고**기 (meat) → "ko-gi" | "g": 고**기** → "ko-gi" | ㄲ: 학**교** (school) [학꾜] |
| ㄷ | soft "t": **다**리 (leg / bridge) → "ta-ri" | "d": 바**다** (sea) → "pa-da" | ㄸ: 식**당** (restaurant) [식땅] |
| ㅂ | soft "p": **비** (rain) → "pi" | "b": 두**부** (tofu) → "tu-bu" | ㅃ: 학**비** (tuition) [학삐] |
| ㅈ | soft "ch": **지**금 (now) → "chi-geum" | "j": 여**자** (woman) → "yeo-ja" | ㅉ: 잡**지** (magazine) [잡찌] |

고기 shows both at once: the first ㄱ sounds like "k" and the second like "g", but it's the same letter. This is also why romanization is inconsistent: 부산 is *Busan* today but was *Pusan* in older romanization, and 김 is still written *Kim*. (The third column is the tensing rule, see [Tensing](#4-tensing-경음화).)

**At the start of a word, plain ≠ aspirated.** The plain consonants sound close to **English** "k/t/p/ch", but they are **not** the same as ㅋ/ㅌ/ㅍ/ㅊ. They sit in between: a little breath, but not the full burst. English speakers hear them as "k/t/p/ch" because English has only two slots for these sounds, and Korean has three.

| Plain | Aspirated | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| **달** (moon) | **탈** (mask) | 탈 has a strong puff of air and a higher starting pitch |
| **불** (fire) | **풀** (grass) | 풀 has a strong puff and a higher pitch; 불 has only a light breath and a lower pitch |
| **자다** (to sleep) | **차다** (to kick) | same pattern |

In Seoul speech, words beginning with an **aspirated or tense** consonant (or ㅅ, ㅎ) start on a **higher pitch** than words with a plain consonant. For many younger speakers this pitch difference is now the main thing that tells 달 and 탈 apart.

**Practical tip:** if your 달 comes out sounding like 탈, start 달 on a **lower, relaxed pitch** with less air, and start 탈 **higher**, with a clear puff. Holding a hand in front of your mouth helps you feel the difference.

### The other consonants

| Letter | Sound | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| ㄴ | n | |
| ㅁ | m | |
| ㅇ | silent / ng | silent at the start of a block, "ng" at the end: 앙 [ang] |
| ㅎ | h | often weakens or disappears between voiced sounds (see below) |
| ㄹ | r / l | a quick **flap** (like the *tt* in American *butter*) between vowels or at the start of a syllable: 나라 (country); a clear **l** at the end of a syllable or when doubled: 발 (foot), 빨래 (laundry) |
| ㅅ / ㅆ | s | before **ㅣ** or a y-vowel it becomes "sh": 시 [shi], 쉬 |

## Vowels

| Letter | Sound (approx.) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| ㅏ | "a" in *father* | |
| ㅓ | "o" in *hot* (British) / "u" in *but*, but open | lips **unrounded**; the hardest distinction for learners is ㅓ vs ㅗ |
| ㅗ | "o" in *go*, no glide | lips **rounded** |
| ㅜ | "oo" in *food* | |
| ㅡ | "oo" with the lips spread flat | no English equivalent; also the "filler" vowel in loanwords: 버스 (bus), 아이스크림 (ice cream) |
| ㅣ | "ee" in *see* | |
| ㅐ / ㅔ | "e" in *bed* | **sound the same** for most speakers today; learn which one each word uses by spelling |
| ㅚ / ㅙ / ㅞ | "we" in *wet* | all three have **merged** in everyday speech |
| ㅟ | "wee" | |
| ㅢ | ㅡ + ㅣ glided | pronounced several ways; see below |

The y-vowels (ㅑ ㅕ ㅛ ㅠ ㅒ ㅖ) are the same vowels with a **y-** in front. After a consonant, **ㅖ** is usually just [ㅔ]: 시계 [시게] (clock). (Exceptions: 예, 례.)

### The four faces of ㅢ

| Position | Pronounced | Example |
|---|---|---|
| start of a word | [의] | 의사 [의사] (doctor) |
| after a consonant | [이] | 희망 [히망] (hope) |
| not first syllable | [이] (or [의]) | 회의 [회이] (meeting) |
| the particle 의 ('s) | [에] (or [의]) | 나의 [나에] (my) |

## Final consonants (받침)

At the end of a syllable, consonants are **not released**. Your mouth closes into position and stops without the puff. Because of this, the 27 possible 받침 spellings collapse into just **7 sounds**:

| Sound | Spelled with | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| [ㄱ] k | ㄱ, ㄲ, ㅋ, ㄳ, ㄺ | 책 (book) [책], 밖 (outside) [박], 부엌 (kitchen) [부억] |
| [ㄴ] n | ㄴ, ㄵ, ㄶ | 산 (mountain), 앉다 (to sit) [안따] |
| [ㄷ] t | ㄷ, ㅅ, ㅆ, ㅈ, ㅊ, ㅌ, ㅎ | 옷 (clothes) [옫], 낮 (daytime) [낟], 꽃 (flower) [꼳], 밭 (field) [받] |
| [ㄹ] l | ㄹ, ㄼ, ㄽ, ㄾ, ㅀ | 물 (water), 여덟 (eight) [여덜] |
| [ㅁ] m | ㅁ, ㄻ | 봄 (spring), 삶 (life) [삼] |
| [ㅂ] p | ㅂ, ㅍ, ㅄ, ㄿ | 밥 (rice, meal), 앞 (front) [압], 값 (price) [갑] |
| [ㅇ] ng | ㅇ | 강 (river) |

So 낫 (sickle), 낮 (daytime), 낯 (face), and 낟 (grain) all sound like **[낟]** when said alone.

**Double finals (겹받침):** usually the **first** consonant is pronounced (앉다 (to sit) [안따], 값 (price) [갑]), but with **ㄺ, ㄻ, ㄿ** the **second** one is (닭 (chicken) [닥], 삶 (life) [삼]). There are a few exceptions (밟다 (to step on) [밥따], 읽고 (read and…) [일꼬]) that you just learn.

## Sound-change rules

These apply **across syllable boundaries**, both inside a word and between a word and its particle or ending. They are the reason written Korean and spoken Korean look different.

### 1. Linking (연음): 받침 moves into the next syllable

If a 받침 is followed by a syllable starting with silent **ㅇ**, the 받침 slides over to fill that empty slot. This is Korean's version of French liaison, but it's **always** applied.

| Written | Pronounced |
|---|---|
| 음악 (music) | [으막] |
| 한국어 (Korean language) | [한구거] |
| 옷이 (clothes + subject particle) | [오시] (the real ㅅ comes back, not [ㄷ]) |
| 읽어요 (I read) | [일거요] (with double 받침, the second one moves) |
| 앉아요 (I sit) | [안자요] |

Two exceptions: **ㅇ** never moves (강아지 (puppy) stays [강아지]), and **ㅎ** disappears instead of moving (좋아요 (it's good) [조아요]).

### 2. Nasalization (비음화)

A [ㄱ], [ㄷ], or [ㅂ] sound before **ㄴ** or **ㅁ** turns into the matching nasal: **ㄱ→ㅇ, ㄷ→ㄴ, ㅂ→ㅁ**.

| Written | Pronounced |
|---|---|
| 감사합니다 (thank you) | [감사함니다] |
| 한국말 (Korean language) | [한궁말] |
| 끝나다 (to end) | [끈나다] |
| 십만 (100,000) | [심만] |

You'll hear this in every formal **-ㅂ니다 / -습니다** ending: [-ㅁ니다 / -슴니다].

Related: **ㄹ** after ㅁ or ㅇ becomes [ㄴ]: 종로 (Jongno, a Seoul district) [종노], 심리 (psychology) [심니]. After ㄱ or ㅂ, both sounds shift: 국립 (national) [궁닙], 독립 (independence) [동닙].

### 3. ㄹ-assimilation (유음화)

**ㄴ + ㄹ** or **ㄹ + ㄴ** becomes **[ㄹㄹ]**.

| Written | Pronounced |
|---|---|
| 신라 (Silla) | [실라] |
| 연락 (contact) | [열락] |
| 설날 (New Year) | [설랄] |
| 한류 (Korean wave) | [할류] |

### 4. Tensing (경음화)

After a [ㄱ], [ㄷ], or [ㅂ] 받침, a following plain **ㄱ ㄷ ㅂ ㅅ ㅈ** becomes tense (**ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅆ ㅉ**).

| Written | Pronounced |
|---|---|
| 학교 (school) | [학꾜] |
| 식당 (restaurant) | [식땅] |
| 없다 (to not exist / not have) | [업따] |
| 잡지 (magazine) | [잡찌] |

Tensing also happens after the future modifier **-(으)ㄹ** (할 거예요 (I will do it) [할꺼예요], 갈 수 있어요 (I can go) [갈쑤이써요]) and inside many compound words (e.g. 물병 (water bottle) [물뼝]), where it has to be learned word by word.

### 5. Aspiration (격음화)

**ㅎ** next to **ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ, ㅈ** (on either side) merges with it into the aspirated sound **ㅋ, ㅌ, ㅍ, ㅊ**.

| Written | Pronounced |
|---|---|
| 좋다 (to be good) | [조타] |
| 많다 (to be many) | [만타] |
| 축하해요 (congratulations) | [추카해요] |
| 입학 (school entrance) | [이팍] |
| 따뜻하다 (warm) | [따뜨타다] |

### 6. ㅎ weakening

Between vowels, or after ㄴ, ㄹ, ㅁ, ㅇ, **ㅎ** is weak and often dropped in normal speech.

| Written | Pronounced |
|---|---|
| 좋아요 (it's good) | [조아요] |
| 싫어요 (I don't like it) | [시러요] |
| 전화 (phone) | [저놔] |
| 은행 (bank) | [으냉] |

### 7. Palatalization (구개음화)

A **ㄷ** or **ㅌ** 받침 followed by **이** becomes **[ㅈ]** or **[ㅊ]**.

| Written | Pronounced |
|---|---|
| 같이 (together) | [가치] |
| 굳이 (insistently) | [구지] |
| 해돋이 (sunrise) | [해도지] |

### 8. ㄴ-insertion (ㄴ 첨가)

In compounds or tightly linked phrases, when the first part ends in a consonant and the second starts with **이** or a y-vowel, an extra **[ㄴ]** is often inserted (then other rules may apply to it).

| Written | Pronounced |
|---|---|
| 한여름 (midsummer) | [한녀름] |
| 무슨 일 (what's the matter) | [무슨닐] |
| 꽃잎 (petal) | [꼰닙] |

## Stress and rhythm

Korean has **no word stress**. No syllable in a word is reliably louder or longer, and every syllable gets roughly equal time. That's a big difference from English, and closer to French.

Pitch is set per **phrase** instead: in Seoul speech a short phrase typically rises toward its end (low … high). Speaking syllable by syllable with an English-style stress on one syllable (e.g. "한-**국**-어") is one of the most noticeable foreign accents.

## Intonation

Intonation does a lot of work in Korean, because the everyday polite ending **-아요/어요** is **the same for statements, questions, commands, and suggestions**. The pitch at the end of the sentence decides which it is:

- **Statement (↘):** 가요. ↘ (I'm going.)
- **Question (↗):** 가요? ↗ (Are you going?)
- **Command / suggestion (firm, level or falling):** 빨리 가요! (Go quickly! / Let's go quickly!)

Question words can flip meaning with intonation too:

- 뭐 먹어요? ↘ → **What** are you eating? (뭐 = "what")
- 뭐 먹어요? ↗ → Are you eating **something**? (뭐 = "something", yes/no question)

The same goes for 누가 (who / someone), 어디 (where / somewhere), 언제 (when / sometime).
