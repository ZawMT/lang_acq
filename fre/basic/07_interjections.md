# Interjections (Les interjections)

An *interjection* expresses a sudden feeling and often stands on its own (see [Interjection](./03_classes.md#interjection)). Like in English, they don't fit into the grammar of the sentence around them the way other word classes do.

## Primary vs. secondary interjections

- **Primary interjections** — words that exist *only* to be interjections; they have no other grammatical job.
  - Example: **ah**, **oh**, **aïe**, **euh**, **ouf**, **hein**, **bof**, **chut**
- **Secondary interjections** — ordinary words (verbs, nouns, adverbs) borrowed to work as an interjection.
  - Example: **tiens !** (from *tenir*, "to hold" — used for surprise, "well, well"), **allez !** (from *aller*, "to go" — used to encourage, "come on!"), **voyons !** (from *voir*, "to see" — used to scold gently, "come now"), **dis donc !** (from *dire* + *donc* — used for surprise, "well I never")

## Interjections by feeling

| Feeling | Examples | Example sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Joy / excitement | youpi, super, génial | **Youpi !** On a gagné ! (Yay! We won!) |
| Surprise | oh, quoi, ah bon | **Quoi !** Je ne m'attendais pas à ça. (What! I didn't expect that.) |
| Pain | aïe, ouille | **Aïe !** Ça fait mal. (Ouch! That hurt.) |
| Disgust | beurk, pouah | **Beurk**, ça sent mauvais. (Ugh, this smells terrible.) |
| Greeting | salut, coucou, allô | **Coucou !** Ça faisait longtemps. (Hey! Long time no see.) |
| Approval / agreement | bravo, chapeau, super | **Bravo !** C'était un excellent spectacle. (Bravo! That was a great show.) |
| Attention / command | eh, regarde, chut | **Chut !** Le bébé dort. (Shh! The baby is sleeping.) |
| Hesitation | euh, ben, hein | **Euh**, je ne suis pas sûr. (Um, I'm not sure about that.) |
| Farewell | salut, à plus, ciao | **Salut !** À demain. (Bye! See you tomorrow.) |

## Punctuation with interjections

The punctuation after an interjection signals how strong the feeling is, same as in English:

- **Strong feeling** → exclamation mark, often standing as its own *phrase*.
  - **Aïe !** Ça fait mal.
- **Mild feeling** → comma, folded into the sentence that follows.
  - **Bah**, on verra bien.

## Points to note for French

### 1. A non-breaking space goes before `!`, `?`, `;`, `:`, and inside `« »`

French typography rule, not specific to interjections but most visible here: a space is inserted **before** `!`, `?`, `;`, and `:` — not just after, like in English. **Aïe !** is correct; **Aïe!** looks like a typo to a French reader. This applies everywhere these marks appear, not only after interjections.

That space is traditionally a **non-breaking space** (*espace insécable*, U+00A0), not an ordinary one. A normal space is a valid point for a line to wrap; a non-breaking space forbids a break there, gluing the two sides together. The reason: `? ! ; :` are two-part marks (a dot plus a stroke, or a stacked pair), so if a line wrapped right after the preceding word, the mark would be left stranded alone at the start of the next line — a lone `!` or `?` floating there doesn't visually read as attached to anything. (Professional typesetting goes further and uses a *thinner* non-breaking space, U+202F — *espace fine insécable* — for these four marks, since a full-width space looks too wide next to them. Plain U+00A0 is the common, good-enough substitute.)

**Guillemets** (« and ») — French's quotation marks — follow the same non-breaking-space logic, but *inside* the marks rather than outside a preceding word: a non-breaking space goes right after `«` and right before `»`, so the opening/closing mark never gets separated from the quoted text by a line break.

- Sentence: Elle a dit : **« Je ne sais pas. »** (She said, "I don't know.")

**The period and comma are excluded from all of this.** `.` and `,` are single, simple marks, so French treats them exactly like English does: no space before, one ordinary space after — no exception, and no non-breaking space involved.

(A related but separate rule: a non-breaking space is often placed **after** a period in an abbreviation — **M. Dupont**, **p. 42** — purely so the abbreviation doesn't get split from the following word across a line break. That's about line-wrapping convenience, not about the period itself needing a space before it.)

### 2. Euphemistic swap-words

French leans heavily on softened stand-ins for vulgar interjections, the same trick as English "darn"/"shoot" for stronger words — but more systematically:

- **zut**, **mince**, **flûte** — all mean roughly "darn," each a polite substitute avoiding a cruder word (*merde*) in front of the wrong company.

### 3. Some interjections are context-dependent, not feeling-dependent

- **salut** works for both "hi" and "bye" — same word, decided entirely by context, the way English overloads "hi"/"bye" less than you'd expect.
- **allô** is a false friend: it looks like "hello" but is used **only** when answering the phone. Using it as a general greeting when meeting someone in person sounds odd/foreign — use *salut* or *bonjour* instead.

### 4. Tag-interjections at the end of a sentence

In casual spoken French, interjections like **hein**, **quoi**, and **non** often get tacked onto the *end* of a sentence to seek agreement or confirmation — functioning like an English tag question ("...right?" / "...huh?") rather than standing alone at the front.

- Sentence: C'est bon, **hein** ? (It's fine, right?)
- Sentence: Il exagère, **non** ? (He's overdoing it, isn't he?)

### 5. Regional flavor: Québécois *sacres*

Metropolitan French swears mostly with body/scatological words (*merde*, *putain*). Québécois French instead has its own family of strong interjections called **sacres**, drawn from religious vocabulary — **tabarnak**, **câlisse**, **ostie**, and combinations of these. They carry a very different register and history from anything in Metropolitan French, so don't assume the two varieties' "strong" interjections are interchangeable or equally strong.
