# Nine Word Classes (Classes grammaticales)

French doesn't use the "eight parts of speech" model. Instead, every word belongs to one of **nine classes grammaticales**, split into two groups: five that change form (**variables**) and four that never change (**invariables**).

## Variables (change form for gender/number/agreement)

### Nom (noun)

Names a person, place, thing, or idea. Has a fixed gender (masculine or feminine) and can be singular or plural.

- Example: **chien**, **Paris**, **bonheur**
- Sentence: Le **chien** court dans le **parc**. (The dog runs in the park.)

### Déterminant (determiner)

Introduces a noun and agrees with it in gender and number. English treats these as a minor add-on to adjectives; French gives them their own full class — articles, possessives, demonstratives, and indefinites all belong here.

- Example: **le**, **la**, **un**, **mon**, **ce**, **quelques**
- Sentence: **Le** chien a mangé **une** pomme. (The dog ate an apple.)

### Pronom (pronoun)

Replaces a noun so it doesn't have to be repeated.

- Example: **il**, **elle**, **le**, **qui**, **celui-ci**
- Sentence: Maria est fatiguée, alors **elle** se repose. (Maria is tired, so she is resting.)

### Adjectif (adjective)

Describes a noun and agrees with it in gender and number.

- Example: **heureux/heureuse**, **bleu**, **grand**
- Sentence: Elle a une voiture **rouge**. (She has a red car.)

### Verbe (verb)

Shows an action or a state of being, and conjugates for person, tense, and mood.

- Example: **courir**, **manger**, **être**, **penser**
- Sentence: Les oiseaux **volent** vers le sud en hiver. (Birds fly south in winter.)

## Invariables (fixed form)

### Adverbe (adverb)

Modifies a verb, an adjective, or another adverb — often answering how, when, where, or to what degree.

- Example: **rapidement**, **très**, **hier**
- Sentence: Il a couru **rapidement** vers l'arrêt de bus. (He ran quickly to the bus stop.)

### Préposition (preposition)

Shows the relationship between a noun (or pronoun) and other words in a sentence — place, time, or direction.

- Example: **dans**, **sur**, **sous**, **après**
- Sentence: Le chat est **sous** la table. (The cat is under the table.)

### Conjonction (conjunction)

Joins words, phrases, or sentences. French doesn't split this into a separate "coordinating vs. subordinating" class the way some English grammars do — both are conjonctions.

- Example: **et**, **mais**, **ou**, **parce que**
- Sentence: Je voulais du thé **mais** nous n'avions que du café. (I wanted tea but we only had coffee.)

### Interjection (interjection)

Expresses a sudden feeling or emotion, often standing on its own.

- Example: **Wow!**, **Aïe!**, **Eh!**
- Sentence: **Wow!** C'est incroyable. (Wow! That's amazing.)

## Why 9 and not 8

French's 9-class model is more consistent than the English 8-part model on one specific point: it never hides a whole category inside another one. English calls "the," "a," and "my" adjectives even though they can't be modified ("very the") or compared ("the-er") — French pulls that entire behavior out into its own class, **déterminant**, so the grouping matches how the words actually function rather than tradition.
