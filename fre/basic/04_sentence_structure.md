# Sentence Structure (Structure de la phrase)

A French **phrase** is built from smaller units nested inside each other, just like an English sentence: *mots* combine into *groupes*, *groupes* combine into *propositions*, and *propositions* combine into a *phrase*.

**Watch the terms — this is the one place French and English grammar vocabulary collide.**

| English | French | Careful |
|---|---|---|
| Sentence | **phrase** | Not the same word as English "phrase"! |
| Phrase (NP, VP, PP...) | **groupe** | *groupe nominal*, *groupe verbal*, *groupe prépositionnel* — never *phrase nominale* |
| Clause | **proposition** | |
| Predicate | **prédicat** (linguistics) / **groupe verbal** (traditional grammar) | |
| Expression / idiom | **expression** / **locution** | *locution* = the fixed-unit grammar term |

## Mot (word)

The smallest meaningful unit. Every word plays one of the [nine word classes](./03_classes.md).

- Example: **chien**, **courir**, **rapidement**

## Groupe (phrase, in the English syntax sense)

A group of related words that acts as a single unit, but does **not** contain both a *sujet* and a verb together.

| Type | Example | Acts like a |
|---|---|---|
| Groupe nominal (GN) | **le gros chien** | nom |
| Groupe verbal (GV) | **a couru** | verbe |
| Groupe adjectival (GAdj) | **très heureux** | adjectif |
| Groupe adverbial (GAdv) | **assez rapidement** | adverbe |
| Groupe prépositionnel (GPrép) | **dans la matinée** | adjectif ou adverbe |

- Sentence: **Le gros chien** a couru **dans la matinée**. (The big dog ran in the morning.)

## Proposition (clause)

A group of words that contains both a **sujet** and a **prédicat** (a verb doing something). A proposition is one level up from a *groupe*.

- **Proposition indépendante** — can stand alone as a complete phrase.
  - Example: **Elle est partie tôt.** (She left early.)
- **Proposition subordonnée** — cannot stand alone; it depends on a proposition principale.
  - Example: **parce qu'elle était fatiguée** (because she was tired) (needs a main clause: "Elle est partie tôt **parce qu'elle était fatiguée**.")

When a proposition subordonnée is attached to another, the one it depends on is called the **proposition principale** (rather than "indépendante") — the terms shift slightly once there's a subordinate clinging to it.

## Prédicat (predicate)

The part of a proposition that says something about the *sujet* — everything except the *sujet*, built around the main verb. Traditional French school grammar usually just calls this the **groupe verbal**; **prédicat** is the more modern/linguistic label for the same thing.

- Sentence: Le chien **a poursuivi le ballon dans le parc**. (The dog chased the ball in the park.)
  - Sujet: Le chien
  - Prédicat (groupe verbal): a poursuivi le ballon dans le parc

**Careful with phrases that have more than one proposition.** "Prédicat" applies per proposition, not just once for the whole sentence. Take: "Elle est partie tôt parce qu'elle était fatiguée."

| Proposition | Sujet | Prédicat |
|---|---|---|
| Elle est partie tôt *(principale)* | Elle | est partie tôt |
| parce qu'elle était fatiguée *(subordonnée)* | elle | était fatiguée |

The proposition subordonnée "parce qu'elle était fatiguée" answers *pourquoi* she left — it modifies the main clause the way an adverb would, rather than being swallowed into the main clause's own *prédicat*.

## Phrase (sentence)

One or more propositions expressing a complete thought, starting with a capital letter and ending with `.`, `!`, or `?`.

| Type | Made of | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | one proposition indépendante | Elle est partie tôt. |
| Coordonnée (compound) | two+ propositions indépendantes joined by a conjunction | Elle est partie tôt, **et** il est resté tard. |
| Complexe | one principale + one or more subordonnées | Elle est partie tôt **parce qu'elle était fatiguée**. |
| Coordonnée-complexe | two+ indépendantes + one or more subordonnées | Elle est partie tôt **parce qu'elle était fatiguée**, **et** il est resté tard. |

### Phrase types by purpose

The table above classifies phrases by their **clause structure**. Phrases can also be classified by **type** — what the phrase is trying to do:

| Type | Purpose | Example | End punctuation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Déclarative (assertive) | makes a statement | Elle est partie tôt. | `.` |
| Interrogative | asks a question | Est-elle partie tôt ? | `?` |
| Impérative (injonctive) | gives a command or request | Pars tôt. | `.` or `!` |
| Exclamative | expresses strong emotion | Elle est partie si tôt ! | `!` |

These two classifications are independent — any type can also be simple, coordonnée, complexe, or coordonnée-complexe. "Est-elle partie tôt parce qu'elle était fatiguée ?" is both **interrogative** and **complexe**.

"Affirmative" and "négative" (**forme**, not **type**) aren't separate phrase types on their own — they describe whether a phrase confirms something or denies it. French negation is two-part, wrapping the verb:

- Affirmative: Elle est partie tôt.
- Négative: Elle **n'**est **pas** partie tôt.

## Expression / Locution

**Expression** is the same informal, everyday term French and English share: any string of words that conveys a meaning — a word, a groupe, an idiom, or a fragment. It isn't a strict grammatical category.

**Locution** is stricter: the grammar term for a fixed multi-word unit that behaves as a single part of speech. You already met these as **locution adverbiale**, **locution conjonctive**, and **locution prépositive** — French treats "parce que" as one conjonction, not two separate words, precisely because it's a locution.

- Example: "**avoir du pain sur la planche**" (an idiomatic expression, grammatically a locution verbale — literally "to have bread on the board," meaning to have a lot on one's plate)

See [Idioms and Fixed Expressions](./05_idioms.md) for more on idioms, fixed expressions, and collocations.

## How they fit together

```
Mot  →  Groupe  →  Proposition (Sujet + Prédicat)  →  Phrase
```

- A **mot** is the smallest piece.
- **Mots** group into a **groupe** (no *sujet*+verb pair of its own).
- A **sujet** plus a **prédicat** (itself often a groupe verbal) forms a **proposition**.
- One or more **propositions** form a **phrase**.
- **Expression** sits outside this ladder — a loose, informal label that can refer to a unit at any level. **Locution** is its stricter cousin, naming a fixed unit that fills one grammatical slot.
