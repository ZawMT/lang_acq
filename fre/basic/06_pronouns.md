# Pronouns (Les pronoms)

A *pronom* replaces a *nom* so we don't have to keep repeating it (see [Pronom](./03_classes.md#pronom-pronoun)). French has more sub-types than English, and several of them change form far more than their English counterparts do.

## Personal pronouns (Pronoms personnels)

English personal pronouns have two forms (subject/object: *he* / *him*). French has **four**, because the direct object, indirect object, and stressed forms are all distinct.

| Personne | Sujet | COD (complément d'objet direct) | COI (complément d'objet indirect) | Tonique |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1st sing. | je | me | me | moi |
| 2nd sing. | tu | te | te | toi |
| 3rd sing. masc. | il | le | lui | lui |
| 3rd sing. fem. | elle | la | lui | elle |
| 1st pl. | nous | nous | nous | nous |
| 2nd pl. | vous | vous | vous | vous |
| 3rd pl. masc. | ils | les | leur | eux |
| 3rd pl. fem. | elles | les | leur | elles |

- Sujet: **Elle** appelle son frère. (She calls her brother.)
- COD: Elle **le** voit. (She sees him.)
- COI: Elle **lui** parle. (She speaks to him/her.)
- Tonique: **Moi**, je pense que... / Il pense à **moi**. (used for emphasis, after prepositions, or standalone)

**Word order is the trap here.** Object pronouns go **before** the conjugated verb in French, not after like in English: *Je **le** vois* ("I him see"), not *"je vois le."* They only jump after the verb in a positive command, joined with a hyphen: *Regarde-**le** !* (Look at him!) — and *me*/*te* become *moi*/*toi* in that position: *Donne-**moi** ça !*, not *"donne-me."*

When a sentence stacks two object pronouns, they follow a fixed order: **me/te/se/nous/vous → le/la/les → lui/leur**.

- Sentence: Il **me le** donne. (He gives it to me.)

## Possessive pronouns (Pronoms possessifs)

Unlike English "mine/yours/his," French possessive pronouns agree in gender and number with the noun they replace — not with the owner.

| Owner | Masc. sing. | Fem. sing. | Masc. pl. | Fem. pl. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| je | le mien | la mienne | les miens | les miennes |
| tu | le tien | la tienne | les tiens | les tiennes |
| il / elle | le sien | la sienne | les siens | les siennes |
| nous | le nôtre | la nôtre | les nôtres | les nôtres |
| vous | le vôtre | la vôtre | les vôtres | les vôtres |
| ils / elles | le leur | la leur | les leurs | les leurs |

- Sentence: Ce livre est **le mien**, pas **le tien**. (This book is mine, not yours.)

**Careful with the accent.** The possessive determiner (*notre livre* — "our book," before a noun) and the possessive pronoun (*le **nôtre*** — "ours," standing alone) are spelled almost identically — the pronoun just adds a circumflex: *notre* / *votre* (determiner) vs. *nôtre* / *vôtre* (pronoun). Easy to drop by accident since it doesn't change pronunciation in speech.

## Reflexive pronouns (Pronoms réfléchis)

Refer back to the subject, showing the action affects the same person/thing that did it. These are required with **verbes pronominaux** (*se laver*, *se lever*, *s'appeler*...) far more often than English uses "-self" verbs.

| Person | Pronoun |
|---|---|
| je | me |
| tu | te |
| il / elle / on | se |
| nous | nous |
| vous | vous |
| ils / elles | se |

- Sentence: Elle **s'**est appris à nager. (She taught herself to swim.)

### Reciprocal meaning reuses these same pronouns

French has **no separate word** for "each other" / "one another." A plural reflexive pronoun does double duty for both reflexive and reciprocal meaning, and context (or an added clarifier) decides which:

- **Ils se regardent.** — ambiguous on its own: "They look at themselves" *or* "They look at each other."
- **Ils se regardent l'un l'autre.** — unambiguous: "They look at each other" (*l'un l'autre* / *les uns les autres* forces the reciprocal reading).

## Demonstrative pronouns (Pronoms démonstratifs)

Point to specific things already mentioned, agreeing in gender and number, with an optional near/far distinction (like *-ci*/*-là* tags on "this one here" / "that one there").

| | Masc. sing. | Fem. sing. | Masc. pl. | Fem. pl. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base | celui | celle | ceux | celles |
| Near (-ci) | celui-ci | celle-ci | ceux-ci | celles-ci |
| Far (-là) | celui-là | celle-là | ceux-là | celles-là |

- Sentence: **Celui-ci** est meilleur que **celui-là**. (This one is better than that one.)

There's also a **neutral** set for an idea or unspecified thing rather than a specific noun: **ceci** (this), **cela** (that), **ça** (informal contraction of *cela*, extremely common in speech).

- Sentence: **Ça** va ? (How's it going? / lit. "That goes?")

## Interrogative pronouns (Pronoms interrogatifs)

| Pronoun | Asks about | Example sentence |
|---|---|---|
| qui | a person | **Qui** a appelé ? (Who called?) |
| que / qu'est-ce que | a thing (object) | **Que** veux-tu ? / **Qu'est-ce que** tu veux ? (What do you want?) |
| quoi | a thing, standalone or after a preposition | À **quoi** penses-tu ? (What are you thinking about?) |
| lequel / laquelle / lesquels / lesquelles | a choice among options — agrees in gender/number | **Lequel** préfères-tu ? (Which one do you prefer?) |

**lequel** contracts with **à** and **de** just like the article does: à + lequel → **auquel**, à + lesquels → **auxquels**; de + lequel → **duquel**, de + lesquels → **desquels**.

## Relative pronouns (Pronoms relatifs)

| Pronoun | Refers to | Example sentence |
|---|---|---|
| qui | subject (people or things) | La femme **qui** a appelé est ma tante. (The woman who called is my aunt.) |
| que | object (people or things) | L'homme **que** tu as rencontré est mon oncle. (The man whom you met is my uncle.) |
| dont | replaces "de + noun" | Le livre **dont** je parle est excellent. (The book I'm talking about is excellent.) |
| où | place or time | La ville **où** j'habite est petite. (The city where I live is small.) |
| lequel / laquelle / ... | object of a preposition — agrees | La chaise sur **laquelle** tu es assis est cassée. (The chair you're sitting on is broken.) |

**dont has no single English equivalent.** It covers "whose," "of which," and "about which" — anywhere the relative clause would otherwise need a stray "de." Learners often try to bolt "que... de" together instead; French wants the single word **dont**.

## Indefinite pronouns (Pronoms indéfinis)

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| People | quelqu'un, personne, chacun / chacune |
| Things | quelque chose, rien, tout |
| Quantity | tous / toutes, certains / certaines, plusieurs, aucun / aucune |
| Generic / impersonal | on |

- Sentence: **Quelqu'un** a apporté **quelque chose**, mais **personne** n'a apporté de gâteau. (Someone brought something, but no one brought a cake.)

**Most indefinite pronouns are invariable, but a few agree in gender.** *Quelqu'un*, *personne*, *quelque chose*, and *rien* never change form. *Chacun/chacune* (each one) and *aucun/aucune* (none, not any) do — they take the gender of whichever noun they stand in for, even though there's no visible noun right next to them in the sentence.

- **Chacune** des filles a apporté son cadeau. (Each of the girls brought her gift — *chacune* is feminine because *les filles* is feminine.)
- Il a lu tous les livres, mais **aucun** ne l'a convaincu. (He read all the books, but none convinced him — *livres* is masculine, so *aucun*.)
- Elle a essayé toutes les robes, mais **aucune** ne lui allait. (She tried on all the dresses, but none fit her — *robes* is feminine, so *aucune*.)

**Negative indefinites need *ne*, like other negation.** *Personne*, *rien*, and *aucun/aucune* aren't negative by themselves — they pair with *ne* the same way *pas* does: *Je **ne** vois **personne**.* (I don't see anyone.) *Il **n'**y a **rien**.* (There is nothing.) *Je **n'**en ai **aucune**.* (I don't have any.)

**"on" deserves special attention.** It's extremely common and covers three different jobs English needs separate words for:

- Informal "we": **On** va au cinéma ce soir. (We're going to the movies tonight.)
- Generic "one" / "people in general": **On** ne sait jamais. (One never knows / You never know.)
- A passive-like construction: **On** parle français ici. (French is spoken here.)

Whichever meaning it carries, **on** always conjugates as 3rd-person singular — even when it means "we."

## En and Y (Pronoms adverbiaux)

These two short words don't correspond to single English pronouns at all — they replace whole prepositional phrases, which is why they're worth treating as their own topic rather than squeezing them into the tables above.

### Y

Replaces **à + a place or à + a thing** (not a person). It also just means "there."

| Replaces | Example |
|---|---|
| à + place | J'habite à Paris. → J'**y** habite. (I live there.) |
| à + thing | Je pense à mon travail. → J'**y** pense. (I think about it.) |
| fixed idiom | **Il y a** un problème. (There is a problem.) |

### En

Replaces **de + noun** — a quantity, a source, or "of/about it/them."

| Replaces | Example |
|---|---|
| de + noun (quantity) | J'ai trois pommes. → J'**en** ai trois. (I have three of them.) |
| de + noun (topic) | Je parle de mon voyage. → J'**en** parle. (I'm talking about it.) |
| de + place (origin) | Je viens de Paris. → J'**en** viens. (I come from there.) |

### Points to note for en/y

1. **They behave like other object pronouns for word order** — before the conjugated verb, except in a positive imperative where they follow with a hyphen: **Vas-y !** (Go on!), **Manges-en !** (Eat some!). Note the extra **-s** grafted onto the normally *s*-less *tu*-imperative (*mange* → *manges-en*) purely to make the liaison sound right — a spelling exception that exists only before *y*/*en*.
2. **Neither can stand for a person.** For "à/de + person," use the tonique pronoun instead: *Je pense à lui* (I think about him), not *"j'y pense"* if *lui* is a person. *Y*/*en* are reserved for things and places.
3. **In a pronoun stack, en and y always come last** — after me/te/se/nous/vous and le/la/les/lui/leur: *Il **m'en** donne.* (He gives me some.) The combo **il y en a** ("there is/are some") is one of the most common phrases in spoken French.
4. **Both show up frozen inside idioms** you'll meet long before you consciously learn the grammar: **il y a** (there is/are; also "ago" — *il y a trois ans*, "three years ago"), **s'en aller** (to leave, go away), **en avoir marre/assez** (to be fed up), **ça y est** (that's it / done).
