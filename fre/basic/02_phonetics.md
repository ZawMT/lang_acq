# Phonetics

French has around 36 phonemes (sounds). Unlike English, the letter-to-sound mapping is mostly rule-governed — the same spelling almost always maps to the same sound — so this file leans on those rules, and reserves IPA for the sounds that don't exist in English or that the rules alone don't explain.

## Consonant sounds

Most French consonants sound close to their English counterparts. The main differences: **r** is pronounced far back in the throat (uvular, not the English tongue-tip r), and **h** is always silent.

| Symbol | Source alphabet(s) | Example word | Example pronunciation |
|---|---|---|---|
| p | p | père | /pɛʁ/ |
| b | b | beau | /bo/ |
| t | t | table | /tabl/ |
| d | d | dans | /dɑ̃/ |
| k | c, k, qu | café, kilo, qui | /kafe/, /kilo/, /ki/ |
| g | g, gu | gare, guerre | /gaʁ/, /gɛʁ/ |
| f | f, ph | famille, photo | /famij/, /fɔto/ |
| v | v | vous | /vu/ |
| s | s, ss, c (before e/i/y), ç | soleil, poisson, cinéma, français | /sɔlɛj/, /pwasɔ̃/, /sinema/, /fʁɑ̃sɛ/ |
| z | z, s (between vowels) | zéro, maison | /zeʁo/, /mɛzɔ̃/ |
| ʃ | ch | chat | /ʃa/ |
| ʒ | j, g (before e/i/y) | jour, genou | /ʒuʁ/, /ʒənu/ |
| m | m | maman | /mamɑ̃/ |
| n | n | non | /nɔ̃/ |
| ɲ | gn | montagne | /mɔ̃taɲ/ |
| l | l | lune | /lyn/ |
| ʁ | r | rouge | /ʁuʒ/ |

`h` is always silent, but it still splits into two behaviors: **h muet** ("mute h") lets liaison and elision happen through it as if it weren't there (l'homme), while **h aspiré** ("aspirated h") blocks them even though it isn't actually pronounced (le héros, not *l'héros). There's no rule to predict which is which from spelling alone — it's a property of each word, usually learned with the word itself.

## Vowel sounds

### Oral vowels

| Symbol | Source alphabet(s) | Example word | Example pronunciation |
|---|---|---|---|
| i | i, î, y | lit, île, stylo | /li/, /il/, /stilo/ |
| e | é, er, ez (word-final) | été, parler, nez | /ete/, /paʁle/, /ne/ |
| ɛ | è, ê, ai, e + pronounced consonant | mère, tête, lait, sept | /mɛʁ/, /tɛt/, /lɛ/, /sɛt/ |
| a | a, à | chat, là | /ʃa/, /la/ |
| ɔ | o (in a closed syllable) | robe | /ʁɔb/ |
| o | ô, au, eau, o (word-final) | hôtel, faute, eau, stylo | /otɛl/, /fot/, /o/, /stilo/ |
| u | ou, où, oû | vous, où, goût | /vu/, /u/, /gu/ |
| y | u, û | tu, sûr | /ty/, /syʁ/ |
| ø | eu, œu (in an open syllable) | peu, œufs (plural) | /pø/, /ø/ |
| œ | eu, œu (in a closed syllable) | peur, œuf (singular) | /pœʁ/, /œf/ |
| ə | e (mute e / schwa) | le, petit | /lə/, /pəti/ |

`y` and `u` (/y/) are the two vowels that give English speakers the most trouble, since English has no equivalent: **y/i** (/i/) is like English "ee," while **u** (/y/) is that same tongue position but with rounded lips — there's no English sound like it.

### Nasal vowels

This is the category with no English equivalent at all: a vowel pronounced with air flowing through the nose as well as the mouth, whenever a vowel is followed by **n** or **m** at the end of a syllable (and that n/m itself is *not* pronounced).

| Symbol | Source alphabet(s) | Example word | Example pronunciation |
|---|---|---|---|
| ɑ̃ | an, am, en, em | dans, chambre, entrer, temps | /dɑ̃/, /ʃɑ̃bʁ/, /ɑ̃tʁe/, /tɑ̃/ |
| ɛ̃ | in, im, ain, ein, (i)en | vin, important, pain, plein, bien | /vɛ̃/, /ɛ̃pɔʁtɑ̃/, /pɛ̃/, /plɛ̃/, /bjɛ̃/ |
| ɔ̃ | on, om | bon, nom | /bɔ̃/, /nɔ̃/ |
| œ̃ | un, um | un, parfum | /œ̃/, /paʁfœ̃/ |

Note the contrast this creates with liaison words: **bon** /bɔ̃/ (nasal) vs. **bonne** /bɔn/ (not nasal — the doubled `n` is pronounced, so the vowel in front of it stays oral). In much of modern spoken French, /œ̃/ is merging into /ɛ̃/, so **un** and **in** are increasingly pronounced the same way.

## Semi-vowels

Covered in [Alphabets](./01_alphabets.md#semi-vowels): the three glide sounds **[j]**, **[ɥ]**, **[w]**, produced when i/y, u, or ou sit next to another vowel and glide into it instead of forming their own syllable.

## e muet (mute e)

The **e** at the end of many words (le, petit, table) represents a schwa /ə/ that is often dropped entirely in ordinary speech, especially when dropping it doesn't create an awkward run of consonants.

- *je ne sais pas* → spoken as **j'sais pas** /ʃsɛpa/
- *petit* said on its own is /pəti/, but in *un petit chat* it's often just /pti/

This is why French speech sounds faster and more "joined together" than the written word-by-word spelling suggests.

## Silent letters and final consonants

Most final consonants in French are silent. The common exception is remembered with the mnemonic **CaReFuL** — **c**, **r**, **f**, and **l** are usually *pronounced* at the end of a word, while other final consonants usually are not.

| Pronounced (CaReFuL) | Example | Silent | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| c | sac /sak/ | s | mois /mwa/ |
| r | mer /mɛʁ/ | t | petit /pəti/ |
| f | chef /ʃɛf/ | z | nez /ne/ |
| l | animal /animal/ | p | beaucoup /boku/ |

Like most rules of thumb, it has exceptions (e.g. the **r** in infinitive **-er** verbs like *parler* is silent), but it covers the large majority of words.

## Liaison

When a word ending in a normally-silent consonant is immediately followed by a word starting with a vowel sound, that consonant is often pronounced, linking the two words together.

| Written | Liaison | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| les amis | s → /z/ | /le.z‿ami/ |
| vous avez | s → /z/ | /vu.z‿ave/ |
| petit ami | t → /t/ | /pə.ti.t‿ami/ |
| un grand homme | d → /t/ | /œ̃.ɡʁɑ̃.t‿ɔm/ |

Liaison is most consistent between a determiner/pronoun and the word right after it (les**‿**amis, nous**‿**avons, ils**‿**ont); it's optional or avoided in many other positions, and this is something learned with practice rather than from a single fixed rule.

## Elision

Certain short, common words drop their final vowel and replace it with an apostrophe when the next word starts with a vowel sound (or a silent/mute h) — this is written, not just spoken.

| Full form | Elided form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| le / la | l' | l'ami, l'école |
| je | j' | j'aime |
| ne | n' | je n'ai pas |
| que | qu' | qu'il vienne |
| si + il | s'il | s'il vous plaît |

## Stress

French does not have variable word stress the way English does — there's no single syllable in a French word that's reliably louder or longer than the rest. Instead, French stress applies to the **rhythmic group** (roughly, a phrase), landing lightly on its *last* syllable.

> Je **VEUX** un ca**FÉ**. — stress falls at the end of the phrase, not on any one "important" word.

This is part of why French can sound smoother/more even to English ears — English pulls out individual stressed words (**I WANT a coffee**), while French spreads emphasis across the whole phrase and only lifts at the end.

## Intonation

French intonation serves similar functions to English, though pitch changes tend to be gentler:

- **Falling tune (↘)** — statements and commands.
  - "J'habite à Paris. ↘"
- **Rising tune (↗)** — yes/no questions asked without inversion (the most common spoken form).
  - "Tu habites à Paris ? ↗"
- **Rising then falling** — wh- questions often rise slightly then fall, the reverse tendency from English.
  - "Où est-ce que tu habites ? ↘"
- **List intonation** — rising on each item except the last, which falls.
  - "J'ai acheté des pommes ↗, des oranges ↗, et des bananes. ↘"
