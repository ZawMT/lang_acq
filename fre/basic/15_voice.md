# Voice (La voix)

Voice tells us whether the subject of a sentence performs the action, or receives it. The active/passive mechanics are close to English's — but French leans on the passive far less often, preferring two constructions of its own instead.

## Voix active (active voice)

The subject **does** the action.

- Structure: Sujet + verbe + objet
- Sentence: **Le chef** a préparé **le repas**. (The chef cooked the meal.)

## Voix passive (passive voice)

The subject **receives** the action. The doer of the action (if mentioned at all) moves into a **par...** phrase at the end.

- Structure: Sujet (= what was the object) + être + participe passé + (par ...)
- Sentence: **Le repas** a été préparé **par le chef**. (The meal was cooked by the chef.)

## Active vs. passive, side by side

| Tense | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| Présent | Elle **écrit** le rapport. | Le rapport **est écrit** par elle. |
| Passé composé | Elle **a écrit** le rapport. | Le rapport **a été écrit** par elle. |
| Imparfait | Elle **écrivait** le rapport. | Le rapport **était écrit** par elle. |
| Futur simple | Elle **écrira** le rapport. | Le rapport **sera écrit** par elle. |

Every passive sentence uses a form of **être** + [participe passé](./13_verb_forms.md#the-building-block-forms), no matter the tense — only the form of *être* changes. **The participle always agrees with the subject**, exactly like [être-auxiliary compound tenses do](./12_verbs_overview.md#le-choix-de-lauxiliaire--avoir-ou-être-auxiliary-choice) — *le rapport... écrit* (masc.) but *la lettre... écrit**e*** (fem.).

## Points to note for French

### 1. French uses the passive noticeably less than English

Where English readily says "the window was broken" or "mistakes were made," French often reaches for one of two alternatives instead — both of which sidestep needing to name (or omit) an agent the way the passive does.

### 2. The "se passif" — reflexive-as-passive

A verb used reflexively can describe something happening to the subject **without an agent at all**, especially for habitual or general-truth statements. This is extremely common for describing how something is typically done, sold, said, etc.

- Sentence: Ce vin **se boit** frais. (This wine is drunk chilled — literally "this wine drinks itself chilled.")
- Sentence: Ça **ne se dit pas**. (That isn't said / you don't say that.)
- Sentence: Ce livre **se vend** bien. (This book sells well / is sold well.)

### 3. "on" as an active stand-in for the passive

*On* (already covered in [Indefinite pronouns](./06_pronouns.md#indefinite-pronouns-pronoms-indéfinis)) frequently replaces a passive construction, turning "X is done" into an active sentence with an unspecified subject:

- Sentence: **On parle** français ici. (French is spoken here — literally "one speaks French here.")
- Sentence: **On m'a dit** que c'était fermé. (I was told it was closed.)

## When to use the true passive

French's actual **voix passive** still shows up, mainly when:

- The doer needs to be named but isn't the focus: **Le tableau a été peint par un artiste inconnu.** (The painting was painted by an unknown artist.)
- The register is formal, journalistic, or scientific: **L'échantillon a été chauffé à 100 °C.** (The sample was heated to 100°C.)
- The action itself is the point, and there's no natural reflexive or *on* alternative: **Le président a été élu hier.** (The president was elected yesterday.)
