# Basic Salutations (Les salutations de base)

An applied note on everyday greetings, farewells, and the polite phrases used to open and close conversations, letters, and emails.

## Greetings by time of day (Salutations selon l'heure)

| Time | Greeting |
|---|---|
| Morning / Afternoon | Bonjour. |
| Evening / Night | Bonsoir. |

**French only splits the day in two, not three.** English separates "good morning" from "good afternoon"; French's **bonjour** covers both, right up until evening. There's no separate French greeting for "afternoon" — reaching for one is a common overcorrection by English speakers who assume French must mirror the three-way split.

## Meeting someone (Faire connaissance)

| Formal (vous) | Informal (tu) |
|---|---|
| Bonjour, comment allez-vous ? | Salut ! Ça va ? |
| Enchanté(e) de faire votre connaissance. | Enchanté(e) ! |
| Comment vous portez-vous ? | Salut, quoi de neuf ? |

**The formal/informal split here isn't just a matter of phrase choice — it's grammatical.** English "you" doesn't change for politeness; French forces a choice between **tu** and **vous** everywhere a 2nd-person pronoun appears, and that choice ripples through the verb's conjugation too (*comment **vas**-tu ?* vs. *comment **allez**-vous ?*). Greetings are exactly where this shows up most visibly and most often. Defaulting to **vous** with strangers, older people, and anyone in a professional context — and reserving **tu** for friends, family, children, and people who've invited you to use it — is the safe rule.

## Farewells (Prendre congé)

| Formal | Informal |
|---|---|
| Au revoir. | Salut ! |
| Passez une bonne journée. | À plus tard ! |
| Ce fut un plaisir de vous rencontrer. | Prends soin de toi ! |
| Bonne nuit. | 'Nuit ! |

Note: **bonne nuit** is used only when parting — leaving someone's company in the evening, or right before bed — the same restriction as English "good night," never a mid-conversation greeting.

## Addressing people (titles)

| Title | Used for |
|---|---|
| Monsieur (M.) | any man |
| Madame (Mme) | an adult woman |
| Docteur (Dr.) | a person with a doctorate/medical degree |
| Monsieur / Madame | someone whose name you don't know (doubles as "Sir"/"Madam") |

- Sentence: Excusez-moi, **Monsieur**, puis-je vous poser une question ?

**"Mademoiselle" is fading out of official use.** It traditionally marked an unmarried woman (like English "Miss"), but the French administration formally dropped it from government paperwork in 2012 after sustained criticism that it forced women to publicly disclose marital status in a way "Monsieur" never asks of men. In everyday and formal French today, **Madame** is increasingly the default for any adult woman regardless of marital status — mirroring the same shift English made toward "Ms." **Mademoiselle** still shows up informally (especially for clearly young girls), but leaning on it in a formal or professional context is now often seen as outdated or presumptuous.

## Written salutations (letters and emails)

| Formal | Informal |
|---|---|
| Madame, Monsieur, | Salut Jean, |
| Cher Monsieur Dupont, | Coucou, |
| Madame la Directrice, | Bonjour, |

Note: when the recipient's name is unknown, a formal French letter opens with the bare **Madame, Monsieur,** — no "Cher/Chère" attached, unlike English's "Dear Sir/Madam." Adding "Cher" to an unnamed recipient reads as oddly familiar.

## Written closings (letters and emails)

| Formal | Informal |
|---|---|
| Je vous prie d'agréer, Madame, Monsieur, l'expression de mes salutations distinguées. | Bises, |
| Veuillez agréer, Madame, Monsieur, mes salutations distinguées. | À bientôt, |
| Cordialement, | Amitiés, |

**This is the single biggest culture-shock point in the whole file.** English formal closings are short — "Sincerely," "Best regards" — but traditional French formal letters close with an entire elaborate sentence, literally something like "I beg you to accept, Madam, Sir, the expression of my distinguished salutations." It isn't optional flourish; omitting it (or replacing it with something as short as an English closing) can read as curt or even rude in a genuinely formal letter — to a government office, a landlord, a lawyer. That said, **Cordialement** alone has become the accepted, much shorter norm for everyday professional email, so it's not necessary to reach for the full formula outside genuinely formal correspondence.
