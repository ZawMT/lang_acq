# Tenses (Les temps)

English organizes its 12 tenses along two independent axes — time (past/present/future) and aspect (simple/continuous/perfect/perfect continuous). **French has no grammaticalized continuous aspect at all** — there's no French verb ending that means what "-ing" means in "I am walking." Instead, French's real aspectual distinction lives entirely inside the past tense, between **imparfait** (ongoing/background/habitual) and **passé composé** (a completed event) — this single contrast does a lot of the work English spreads across simple vs. continuous vs. perfect.

## Present tense (Le présent)

One tense covers what English splits into up to three: simple present, present continuous, and — with **depuis** — even part of the present perfect continuous.

| Use | French | English equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| Habitual/general fact | Elle **travaille** ici. | She works here. |
| Happening right now | Elle **travaille** en ce moment. | She is working right now. |
| Ongoing since a point in time | Elle **travaille** ici **depuis** trois ans. | She **has been working** here for three years. |

**The last row is the one that trips people up.** English reaches for the present perfect continuous ("has been working") for an action that started in the past and is still true now; French just uses the plain **présent** + **depuis**, never a compound past tense, for this meaning. Using a past tense here (*"elle a travaillé depuis trois ans"*) is a direct translation error — it would imply the working has stopped.

For genuine emphasis on "in the middle of doing something," French can optionally add the periphrasis **être en train de** + infinitive (*Elle **est en train de travailler**.*), but this is optional color, not a required grammatical form the way "-ing" is in English.

## Past tenses (Les temps du passé)

| Tense | Built from | Example sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Passé composé | avoir/être (présent) + participe passé | Elle **a travaillé** hier. (She worked yesterday.) |
| Imparfait | stem + imperfect endings (-ais, -ais, -ait, -ions, -iez, -aient) | Elle **travaillait** tous les jours. (She used to work every day.) |
| Plus-que-parfait | avoir/être (imparfait) + participe passé | Elle **avait travaillé** avant midi. (She had worked before noon.) |
| Passé simple | stem + literary past endings | Elle **travailla** ce jour-là. (She worked that day.) — literary/formal writing only, essentially never spoken |

### Imparfait vs. passé composé — the distinction that matters most

This is the single most important tense choice in French, and it doesn't map onto any English tense choice directly:

- **Imparfait** — sets the scene: an ongoing state, a habit, background description. "What was going on."
- **Passé composé** — reports an event: something that happened, with a clear start/end. "What happened."

- Sentence: Il **pleuvait** (imparfait — background weather) quand elle **est arrivée** (passé composé — the event that interrupted it). (It was raining when she arrived.)
- Sentence: Quand j'**étais** enfant (imparfait — ongoing state), j'**habitais** à Paris (imparfait — habitual). Un jour, nous **avons déménagé** (passé composé — a one-time event) à Lyon.

English speakers often default to passé composé for everything since it maps most obviously onto "happened" — but overusing it where imparfait belongs makes background description sound like a sequence of sudden events.

## Temps littéraires (literary-only tenses)

Beyond the passé simple already mentioned above, French has a small family of tenses reserved almost exclusively for formal writing and literature. Native speakers recognize them from reading but essentially never produce them in speech — the everyday equivalent is used instead, every time.

| Literary tense | Everyday spoken equivalent | Example (literary) |
|---|---|---|
| Passé simple | Passé composé | Elle **partit**. (vs. spoken: Elle **est partie**.) |
| Passé antérieur | Plus-que-parfait | Dès qu'elle **eut fini**, elle partit. (vs. spoken: Dès qu'elle **avait fini**, elle est partie.) |
| Imparfait du subjonctif | Subjonctif présent | Il fallait qu'elle **partît**. (vs. spoken: Il fallait qu'elle **parte**.) |
| Plus-que-parfait du subjonctif | Subjonctif passé | Il aurait fallu qu'elle **fût partie**. (vs. spoken: Il aurait fallu qu'elle **soit partie**.) |

*Passé antérieur* pairs with *passé simple* in literary narration the same way *plus-que-parfait* pairs with *passé composé* in everyday narration — same relationship, one register up. Treat this whole table as **recognition-only**: worth being able to read, never a priority to learn producing.

## Future tenses (Les temps du futur)

| Tense | Built from | Example sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Futur proche | aller (présent) + infinitif | Elle **va travailler** demain. (She's going to work tomorrow.) |
| Futur simple | infinitif stem + future endings (-ai, -as, -a, -ons, -ez, -ont) | Elle **travaillera** demain. (She will work tomorrow.) |
| Futur antérieur | avoir/être (futur simple) + participe passé | Elle **aura travaillé** avant midi. (She will have worked before noon.) |

**Futur proche is the everyday spoken future** — the direct structural parallel to English "going to," right down to using the same verb of motion (*aller* / "go"). **Futur simple** is more common in writing and for more distant/formal predictions, roughly like choosing "will" over "going to" in English, but the French split is sharper and more consistently observed in speech than the English one is.

## Quick comparison with English

| French tense | Nearest English equivalent | Where they diverge |
|---|---|---|
| Présent | simple present / present continuous | one French form covers both; + *depuis* also covers "have been doing" |
| Passé composé | simple past / present perfect | French doesn't distinguish "I worked" from "I have worked" — passé composé covers both |
| Imparfait | past continuous / "used to" | no single English tense matches; it's French's own aspectual category |
| Futur proche | "going to" future | near-exact parallel |
| Futur simple | "will" future | near-exact parallel, more formal/written |
