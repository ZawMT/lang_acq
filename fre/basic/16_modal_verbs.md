# Modal-type Verbs (Les verbes modaux)

English modal verbs (can, must, should...) are a **closed, invariant** class — they never conjugate, never take "-s," never have an infinitive form. **French has no such class.** The job is done instead by a handful of ordinary verbs — **pouvoir, devoir, vouloir, savoir, falloir** — that conjugate completely normally across every person and tense, the same as [any other verb](./12_verbs_overview.md#main-vs-auxiliary-verbs-verbes-principaux-vs-auxiliaires). French grammar calls them **semi-auxiliaires** precisely because they're auxiliary-*like* in function but not a distinct grammatical category the way English modals are.

## The core semi-auxiliary verbs

| Verb | Main use | Example sentence |
|---|---|---|
| pouvoir | ability, permission, possibility (circumstantial) | Elle **peut** nager. (She can swim [she's able to/allowed to, right now].) |
| devoir | obligation, probability | Tu **dois** partir. (You must leave.) Elle **doit** être fatiguée. (She must be tired [probability].) |
| vouloir | want, willingness | Je **veux** partir. (I want to leave.) |
| savoir | ability (learned skill) | Je **sais** nager. (I know how to swim [I have the skill].) |
| falloir | impersonal necessity — only ever used as **il faut** | **Il faut** partir. (One must leave / It's necessary to leave.) |

Because these are full verbs, they conjugate — *pouvoir* alone gives *je peux, tu peux, il peut, nous pouvons, vous pouvez, ils peuvent* — something no English modal ever does.

## Points to note for French

### 1. "Can" splits into two different French verbs

English "can" covers both circumstantial ability/permission *and* learned skill with one word. French keeps these separate:

- **pouvoir** — able to right now, due to circumstance or permission: *Je **peux** conduire ce soir.* (I can drive tonight [I have a car / I'm not drinking].)
- **savoir** — able to because you've learned how: *Je **sais** conduire.* (I know how to drive [I have the skill].)

Mixing them up is a common, meaning-changing error: *"Je sais conduire ce soir"* would oddly imply you just learned how to drive tonight specifically, not that you're free to drive.

### 2. Nuance shifts by tense/mood, not by swapping words

English softens or reshapes a modal's meaning by picking a **different word** (can → could, must → should, will → would). French keeps the **same verb** and shifts its tense or mood instead — a genuinely different mechanism worth internalizing:

| English pair | French: same verb, different tense/mood |
|---|---|
| must → should | devoir (présent): **Tu dois** venir. → devoir (conditionnel): **Tu devrais** venir. |
| can → could (polite/hypothetical) | pouvoir (présent): **Je peux** venir. → pouvoir (conditionnel): **Je pourrais** venir. |
| will → would | vouloir (présent): **Je veux** du thé. → vouloir (conditionnel): **Je voudrais** du thé. (I would like tea — the standard polite request) |

### 3. *falloir* only ever takes the impersonal "il"

Unlike every other verb on this list, *falloir* has no personal forms at all — it's never *"je faux"* or *"tu faux."* It's always **il faut**, with the person who has the obligation expressed separately if needed: *Il **me** faut partir.* (I need to leave — literally "it is necessary to me to leave.")

## Degrees of certainty (degree of confidence)

| Confidence | French | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Certain | devoir (présent) | Elle **doit** être chez elle — les lumières sont allumées. (She must be home.) |
| Likely | devoir (conditionnel) | Elle **devrait** être rentrée maintenant. (She should be home by now.) |
| Possible | pouvoir | Elle **peut** déjà être rentrée. (She may already be home.) |
| Unlikely | ça m'étonnerait que + subjonctif | **Ça m'étonnerait qu'**elle soit déjà rentrée. (I'd be surprised if she's already home.) |
| Impossible | ne peut pas | Elle **ne peut pas** être chez elle — je viens de la voir au travail. (She can't be home.) |
