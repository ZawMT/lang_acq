# Idioms and Fixed Expressions (Expressions idiomatiques et locutions figées)

These are groups of words that native speakers use as a ready-made unit, rather than building fresh from grammar rules each time. See also [Expression / Locution](./04_sentence_structure.md#expression--locution) for how this fits alongside *mots*, *groupes*, *propositions*, and *phrases*.

## Idiomes (idioms)

An idiom (*idiotisme*, more often just called *expression idiomatique*) is a phrase whose meaning **can't be worked out from the individual words**. You have to learn the whole phrase as one unit — and translating it word-for-word into English produces nonsense.

| Idiome | Literal words | Meaning | Example sentence |
|---|---|---|---|
| avoir le cafard | to have the cockroach | to feel down/blue | Depuis son départ, elle **a le cafard**. |
| poser un lapin à quelqu'un | to put a rabbit on someone | to stand someone up | Il **m'a posé un lapin** hier soir. |
| tomber dans les pommes | to fall into the apples | to faint | Elle **est tombée dans les pommes** de chaleur. |
| avoir un chat dans la gorge | to have a cat in the throat | to have a frog in one's throat | Excusez-moi, **j'ai un chat dans la gorge**. |
| coûter les yeux de la tête | to cost the eyes of the head | to cost an arm and a leg | Cette voiture **coûte les yeux de la tête**. |
| avoir le coup de foudre | to have the lightning strike | love at first sight | Ils **ont eu le coup de foudre** en se rencontrant. |

## Locutions figées (fixed expressions)

A *locution figée* is a set phrase that **is** understandable from its words, but is still used as a fixed formula — especially for politeness, opinion, or connecting ideas. Unlike *idiomes*, you could technically guess the meaning; you just wouldn't naturally phrase it any other way.

| Locution | Function | Example sentence |
|---|---|---|
| à mon avis | polite way to give an opinion | **À mon avis**, ce n'est pas une bonne idée. |
| au fait | introduce a side note | **Au fait**, tu l'as rappelée ? |
| autant que je sache | soften a statement's certainty | **Autant que je sache**, le magasin est ouvert. |
| à vrai dire | signal a frank opinion | **À vrai dire**, je n'ai pas aimé le film. |
| cela dit | offer an opinion humbly / concede a point | **Cela dit**, tu n'as pas tort. |
| en d'autres termes | rephrase for clarity | Elle est occupée — **en d'autres termes**, non. |

## Collocations

A related but distinct idea: a collocation is simply two or more words that habitually go together, even though each word keeps its normal meaning. Getting these "wrong pairings" is a common sign of a non-native speaker, even when the grammar is correct.

- **prendre** une décision *(not "faire une décision")*
- **poser** une question *(not "demander une question" — demander means to ask for/request something)*
- **il pleut des cordes** *("it's raining ropes," not "il pleut fort")*

## Points to note for French

### 1. *Avoir* where English uses "to be"

This is the single biggest trap, and it's systematic rather than a one-off collocation quirk: French uses **avoir** (to have) for many physical/mental states that English expresses with "to be." Literally translating "I am hungry" as *"je suis faim"* is simply wrong grammar, not just unidiomatic.

| French (avoir) | Literal | English (to be) |
|---|---|---|
| avoir faim / soif | to have hunger / thirst | to be hungry / thirsty |
| avoir chaud / froid | to have hot / cold | to be hot / cold |
| avoir peur | to have fear | to be afraid |
| avoir raison / tort | to have reason / wrong | to be right / wrong |
| avoir sommeil | to have sleepiness | to be sleepy |
| avoir 20 ans | to have 20 years | to be 20 years old |

### 2. Frozen grammar inside fixed expressions

Some *locutions figées* preserve grammar or vocabulary no longer used anywhere else in modern French — you can't reverse-engineer them from today's rules.

- **quoi qu'il en soit** ("be that as it may") — uses the subjunctive of *être* (*soit*), frozen in place.
- **coûte que coûte** ("whatever it costs" / "at all costs") — subjunctive of *coûter* used twice, a construction that doesn't occur elsewhere.
- **avoir maille à partir (avec quelqu'un)** ("to have a bone to pick") — *maille* here is an archaic word for a small coin; it doesn't exist in modern French outside this one phrase.

### 3. Fixed expressions resist modification

Unlike a normal phrase, you generally can't pluralize, negate, or insert words into a *locution figée* without breaking it or changing the meaning. "Poser un lapin" stays singular even for repeated no-shows; you can't say *"il m'a posé un gros lapin"* and expect it to still mean "stood me up" (it would just sound like a mistake, not a bigger idiom).

### 4. Register (*registre de langue*) matters more than in English

French draws harder lines between **soutenu** (formal/literary), **courant** (neutral), **familier** (casual), and **argot** (slang) than English usually does — and idioms are heavily marked for register. Using a *familier* idiom in a formal letter (or a *soutenu* one with friends) reads as odd in a way that's more jarring than the English equivalent.

- avoir la flemme (*familier* — "can't be bothered") vs. manquer d'entrain (*soutenu* — "lack motivation")
- bosser (*familier* — "to work/grind") vs. travailler (*courant*)

### 5. Regional variation

Idioms diverge sharply by region — more so than most everyday vocabulary. An idiom from Québec can be unrecognizable in France, and vice versa.

- Québec: **avoir de la broue dans le toupet** ("to have foam in one's bangs," i.e. to be overwhelmed/swamped) — not used in France.
- Belgium: **faire la file** ("to do the line") where France says *faire la queue* (queue up).

If you're building content aimed at Metropolitan French, it's worth tagging any idiom that's actually regional so learners aren't surprised when it doesn't land elsewhere.

### 6. Proverbes are a different category

A *proverbe* is a complete, traditional sentence carrying a moral or piece of wisdom — not a phrase-level building block like an idiome or locution. Don't conflate the two: an idiome slots into a sentence you're building; a proverbe is the whole sentence, quoted as-is.

- **Petit à petit, l'oiseau fait son nid.** ("Little by little, the bird builds its nest")
- **Qui vole un œuf vole un bœuf.** ("Who steals an egg steals an ox")
