# Nouns (Les noms)

A *nom* names a person, place, thing, or idea (see [Nom](./03_classes.md#nom-noun)). Nouns can be classified in several independent ways — a single noun can belong to more than one category at once. French shares all of English's classification axes below, but adds one English doesn't have at all: **grammatical gender**.

## Genre grammatical (grammatical gender)

Every French noun is inherently **masculine** or **feminine** — even for objects with no natural gender. This isn't optional or skippable: it determines which article, adjective form, and pronoun you must use with that noun everywhere in a sentence. English has nothing equivalent (a "table" isn't gendered in English at all).

| | Masculine | Feminine |
|---|---|---|
| Article | le, un | la, une |
| Example | **le** livre (the book) | **la** table (the table) |

There's no foolproof rule, but common **endings** give a strong hint:

| Often masculine | Often feminine |
|---|---|
| -age, -isme, -ment, -eau | -e, -tion, -sion, -té, -ette |
| le fromage, le tourisme, le gouvernement | la nation, la télévision, la liberté |

For people and animals, many nouns have distinct masculine/feminine forms:

- un acteur / une actrice (actor/actress)
- un chat / une chatte (male cat/female cat)
- un ami / une amie (male friend/female friend)

**A few nouns change meaning entirely depending on gender** — genuinely two different words that happen to be spelled the same:

| Masculine | Meaning | Feminine | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| le livre | the book | la livre | the pound (weight/currency) |
| le tour | the turn / trick | la tour | the tower |
| le poste | the job / position | la poste | the post office |

## Noms communs vs. noms propres (common vs. proper nouns)

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Nom commun | a general name for any member of a class, not capitalized | le chien, la ville, le livre |
| Nom propre | the specific name of one particular person, place, or thing, always capitalized | Rex, Londres, la Bible |

- Sentence: Le **chien** a couru vers **Londres**.

**French capitalizes fewer things than English.** Days of the week, months, languages, and nationalities used as adjectives are **not** capitalized in French, even though their English equivalents are:

- lundi (Monday), janvier (January), français *(as a language/adjective)* — all lowercase.
- Only the noun for a nationality/people stays capitalized: **un Français** (a Frenchman) vs. **la langue française** (the French language, lowercase — it's an adjective here).

## Noms comptables vs. indénombrables (countable vs. uncountable)

This axis is about grammar: can it be counted and made plural?

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Nom comptable | can be counted, has singular/plural, follows a number or *un/une* | une pomme, deux pommes |
| Nom indénombrable | can't be counted or pluralized, uses *du/de la/des* rather than a number | de l'eau, du riz, de l'information |

- Sentence: J'ai **trois pommes**, mais seulement **un peu d'eau**.

The two well-known subtypes carry over directly:

- **Noms de matière (material nouns)** — a substance something is made from.
  - Example: **l'or**, **l'argent**, **le bois**, **le coton**, **le sucre**
  - Sentence: Cette bague est faite d'**or**.
- **Noms abstraits (abstract nouns)** — a quality, feeling, or idea rather than a physical thing (see below).
  - Example: **l'honnêteté**, **le bonheur**, **la liberté**
  - Sentence: **L'honnêteté** est la meilleure politique.

## Noms concrets vs. abstraits (concrete vs. abstract nouns)

This axis is about meaning: can you experience it with your senses?

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Nom concret | names something physical you can see, hear, touch, taste, or smell | la table, le chien, la musique |
| Nom abstrait | names a quality, feeling, idea, or state with no physical form | l'amour, le courage, la liberté |

- Sentence: Son **courage** l'a aidée à gravir la **montagne**.

## Noms collectifs (collective nouns)

A collective noun names a group treated as a single unit — countable (*une équipe, deux équipes*), but grammatically singular when referring to the group as a whole.

| Nom collectif | Group of |
|---|---|
| l'équipe | joueurs |
| la famille | proches |
| le troupeau | bétail/éléphants |
| la foule | personnes |
| le jury | jurés |
| le personnel | employés |

- Sentence: L'**équipe** **joue** bien cette saison.

**French is stricter here than English.** British English sometimes lets a collective noun take a plural verb ("the team *are* playing"), but French never does — a *nom collectif* always takes a singular verb, full stop.

## Noms composés (compound nouns)

A compound noun is made of two or more words joined to name one thing — often with a hyphen, sometimes linked by a preposition.

| Form | Example |
|---|---|
| Noun + noun | le chou-fleur (cauliflower), la grand-mère (grandmother) |
| Verb + noun | le grille-pain (toaster, lit. "grills-bread"), le tire-bouchon (corkscrew) |
| Noun + de + noun | la pomme de terre (potato), la salle de bain (bathroom) |

- Sentence: Elle a acheté un nouveau **grille-pain** et un **chou-fleur**.

**Pluralizing a compound noun depends on what each part is** — a much stricter and more variable rule than English's "just add -s to the whole thing":

- **Noun + noun** — both parts pluralize: un chou-fleur → des **choux-fleurs**.
- **Verb + noun** — the verb never changes (it's not a real plural-able word); only the noun might: un grille-pain → des **grille-pains** (or invariable, depending on the noun's own sense — this one does add -s).
- **Noun + de + noun** — only the first noun pluralizes; the part after *de* stays fixed: une pomme de terre → des **pommes de terre** (never *"pommes de terres"*).

## Points to note for French

### 1. Gender cascades through the whole sentence

Because every noun carries a gender, getting it wrong doesn't just mis-set one word — it forces the wrong article, the wrong adjective ending, and sometimes the wrong pronoun throughout the sentence. It's worth memorizing a noun's gender **together with its article** from day one (*le livre*, not just *livre*) rather than trying to learn nouns and gender separately.

### 2. No possessive 's — French always uses *de*

English's apostrophe-s ("Marie's book") doesn't exist in French. Possession is always expressed with **de** + the owner, in the opposite order from English:

- **le livre de Marie** (Marie's book — literally "the book of Marie")
- **la voiture de mon frère** (my brother's car)

### 3. Silent plural -s

Adding **-s** for the regular plural is usually **silent** in speech — *le livre* / *les livres* sound identical on their own; only the article (*le* → *les*) signals the change out loud. This is unlike English, where most plural -s/-es is audible. A few endings pluralize differently and *are* worth learning as their own small set: **-eau/-eu → -x** (bateau → bateaux), **-al → -aux** (cheval → chevaux), and words already ending in **-s/-x/-z stay unchanged** (une souris → des souris).
