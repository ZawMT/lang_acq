# Verbs Overview (Aperçu des verbes)

A *verbe* shows an action or a state of being (see [Verbe](./03_classes.md#verbe-verb)). Verbs have enough ground to cover that they're split across several files, same as English: [forms](./13_verb_forms.md), [tenses](./14_tenses.md), [voice](./15_voice.md), [modal-type verbs](./16_modal_verbs.md), [mood and conditionals](./17_mood_and_conditionals.md), and [non-finite verbs](./18_non_finite_verbs.md) each get their own file. This overview covers the same functional verb *types* English does, plus four concepts English barely needs but French can't be understood without: **conjugation groups**, **simple vs. compound tenses**, **auxiliary choice (avoir/être)**, and the **four moods**.

## Action vs. stative verbs (Verbes d'action vs. d'état)

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Action (dynamic) verb | describes something being done | courir, manger, écrire, sauter |
| Stative verb | describes a state, feeling, or fact rather than an action | savoir, croire, aimer, posséder, sembler |

- Sentence: Elle **court** tous les matins et **croit** que ça la garde en forme.

**French doesn't police this distinction grammatically the way English does.** English stative verbs reject the continuous form ("I **know** the answer," never "I **am knowing** the answer"), because English has a dedicated progressive aspect (*-ing*) that only action verbs normally take. French has no equivalent obligatory progressive built into its conjugation — the simple present covers both ongoing and habitual meaning for any verb, stative or not (*je sais*, *je cours* both just use the present). When French really wants to stress "in the middle of doing it," it reaches for the periphrasis **être en train de** + infinitive, but nothing forces this the way English forces "-ing" — it's optional emphasis, not a grammatical requirement.

## Transitive vs. intransitive verbs (Verbes transitifs vs. intransitifs)

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Transitive verb | needs a direct object to complete its meaning | Elle a lu **un livre**. |
| Intransitive verb | doesn't take a direct object | Elle dort. |

Some verbs can be either, depending on the sentence: *Elle mange **une pomme**.* (transitive) vs. *Elle mange.* (intransitive) — this distinction matters more in French than in English, because it feeds directly into the past-participle-agreement rule under auxiliary choice, below.

## Linking verbs (Verbes d'état / copules)

A linking verb connects the subject to a complement that describes it, rather than showing an action.

| Examples | Example sentence |
|---|---|
| être, sembler, devenir, paraître, rester | Elle **semble** heureuse. Il **est devenu** médecin. |

## Main vs. auxiliary verbs (Verbes principaux vs. auxiliaires)

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Main (lexical) verb | carries the sentence's real meaning | Elle est en train de **courir**. |
| Auxiliary (helping) verb | supports the main verb to build a compound tense | Elle **a** couru. |

**French has only two core auxiliaries — avoir and être — not three.** There's no French equivalent of English "do-support": French doesn't need a helper verb to form questions or negation. Questions are built by inversion or **est-ce que**, and negation wraps the main verb directly with **ne... pas**, no auxiliary required. (Semi-auxiliary verbs like **devoir**, **pouvoir**, **vouloir**, and **aller** — used for near-future, "futur proche" — behave more like English modals; see [Modal-type verbs](./16_modal_verbs.md).)

## Les trois groupes de conjugaison (the three conjugation groups)

Every French verb belongs to one of three groups, and knowing the group tells you which conjugation pattern to expect.

| Group | Ending | Regularity | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1er groupe | -er | regular (~90% of all French verbs) | parler, aimer, manger |
| 2e groupe | -ir (with an -iss- infix in the plural) | regular | finir → nous fin**iss**ons, ils fin**iss**ent |
| 3e groupe | everything else | irregular | -re verbs (vendre, prendre), leftover -ir verbs (partir, venir), and the core irregulars (être, avoir, aller, faire, pouvoir, vouloir, devoir, savoir...) |

**Don't treat the 3rd group as a small leftover bucket.** It's irregular by definition, but it contains the handful of verbs used constantly in everyday French — *être*, *avoir*, *aller*, *faire*, *pouvoir*, *vouloir*, *devoir*, *prendre*, *venir*, *dire* — so they need to be memorized as core vocabulary early, not treated as rare exceptions to get to later. (Note: *aller*, despite ending in *-er*, is irregular and belongs to the 3rd group, not the 1st.)

## Temps simples vs. temps composés (simple vs. compound tenses)

| Type | Built from | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Temps simple | one word — a stem + ending | il **parle** (present), il **parlait** (imperfect), il **parlera** (future) |
| Temps composé | auxiliary (conjugated) + past participle | il **a parlé** (passé composé), il **avait parlé** (pluperfect), il **aura parlé** (future perfect) |

English has compound tenses too ("has eaten," "will have eaten"), but they're a smaller part of everyday English. **In French, the compound *passé composé* is the everyday spoken past tense** — the "simple" past tense that matches it grammatically, the **passé simple**, has fallen out of spoken use almost entirely and now survives mainly in formal writing and literature. Don't let the word "simple" mislead you into thinking it's the one to prioritize — for speaking, *passé composé* is what you need first.

## Le choix de l'auxiliaire : avoir ou être (auxiliary choice)

Which auxiliary a verb takes in a *temps composé* isn't a free choice — it's fixed per verb, and it changes how the past participle behaves.

| Auxiliary | Used for | Past participle agreement |
|---|---|---|
| **avoir** | most verbs (the default) | doesn't agree with the subject, but **does** agree with a preceding direct object, if there is one |
| **être** | a small fixed set of intransitive movement/state verbs (aller, venir, arriver, partir, entrer, sortir, monter, descendre, naître, mourir, rester, tomber...) | agrees with the **subject**, like an adjective |
| **être** | *all* reflexive/pronominal verbs (se laver, se lever, s'appeler...), no exceptions | agrees with the subject (usually) |

- Elle **est allée** au marché. (She went to the market — *être*, participle agrees: **allée**, feminine.)
- Elle **a mangé** une pomme. (She ate an apple — *avoir*, no subject agreement.)
- Elle **s'est lavée**. (She washed herself — reflexive, always *être*, agrees: **lavée**.)

The direct-object-agreement rule for *avoir* verbs is genuinely one of French's trickiest spelling points — it gets its own full treatment in [Verb forms](./13_verb_forms.md); this section is just the map of *why* it happens (it's tied to which auxiliary the verb takes).

## Les quatre modes (the four moods)

*Mode* (mood) is the organizing axis for everything in [Tenses](./14_tenses.md) and [Mood and conditionals](./17_mood_and_conditionals.md) — nearly every tense you'll learn belongs to one of these four:

| Mood | Expresses | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Indicatif** | facts, real actions — where most tenses live (présent, passé composé, imparfait, futur...) | Elle **part** demain. (She's leaving tomorrow.) |
| **Subjonctif** | doubt, desire, necessity, emotion — triggered by certain verbs/conjunctions (see [Points to note for French](./08_prepositions_conjunctions.md#6-many-subordinating-conjunctions-trigger-the-subjunctive)) | Il faut qu'elle **parte**. (She has to leave.) |
| **Conditionnel** | hypothetical situations, polite requests — roughly French's "would" | Elle **partirait** si elle pouvait. (She would leave if she could.) |
| **Impératif** | direct commands | **Pars** ! (Leave!) |

English blends most of this into modal verbs (would, should, might) and word order, rather than dedicating a distinct conjugated mood to each — French's mood system is a bigger structural commitment, which is why it gets its own dedicated file rather than being folded into tenses.
