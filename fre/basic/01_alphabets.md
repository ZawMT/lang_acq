# Alphabets

French uses the same 26-letter Latin alphabet as English. A few letters (**W**, **K**) are rare and appear mostly in borrowed words (**wagon**, **kilo**), and several letters are pronounced quite differently from their English sounds.

## Vowels

French treats **six** letters as vowels — one more than English, because **Y** counts as a vowel rather than a semi-vowel.

- A
- E
- I
- O
- U
- Y
  E.g. stylo, pays

## Semi-vowels

English assigns semi-vowel status to specific *letters* (W, Y). French doesn't — instead it has three semi-vowel (glide) *sounds*, produced when the vowel letters **i**, **u**, or **ou** sit right next to another vowel and glide into it instead of forming their own syllable:

- **[j]** — from **i** or **y** before a vowel
  E.g. bien, yeux
- **[ɥ]** — from **u** before a vowel
  E.g. huit, lui
- **[w]** — from **ou** before a vowel (also written **oi** = /wa/)
  E.g. oui, moi

So yes, French does have semi-vowels — it just doesn't tie them to dedicated letters the way English ties them to W and Y. It's a sound-based (phonetic) category rather than a letter-based one.

## Special marks (diacritics)

| Mark | Symbol | Appears on | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Accent aigu | é | e only | Marks a closed "ay" sound /e/ | été, café |
| Accent grave | à, è, ù | a, e, u | On **e**, marks an open sound /ɛ/; on **a**/**u**, just distinguishes homophones (a/à, ou/où) | mère, voilà, où |
| Accent circonflexe | â, ê, î, ô, û | a, e, i, o, u | Often marks a letter (usually **s**) that has dropped out of the word historically | château, forêt, île, hôtel, sûr |
| Tréma | ë, ï, ü | e, i, u | Shows two adjacent vowels are pronounced separately, not blended into one sound | Noël, naïve, capharnaüm (a total mess) |
| Cédille | ç | c only | Keeps a "soft" /s/ sound on **c** before **a**, **o**, **u** (where **c** would otherwise be hard, /k/) | français, garçon |
