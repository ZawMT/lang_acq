# Idiomatic Verbs (Les verbes idiomatiques)

English's [phrasal verbs](../../eng/basic/19_phrasal_verbs.md) combine a verb with a small, productive set of **particles** (up, down, off, out...) that can often move around the object ("turn off the light" / "turn the light off"). **French has no real equivalent of this system** — no particle set that combines freely with many verbs, and nothing that moves. What French has instead are two related but distinct categories, plus the *locutions verbales* already introduced in [Idioms](./05_idioms.md#locutions-figées-fixed-expressions).

## Verbes pronominaux idiomatiques (idiomatic reflexive verbs)

The closest French gets to a phrasal-verb *feel* — a reflexive pronoun combined with **en** or **y** ([already covered in Pronouns](./06_pronouns.md#en-and-y-pronoms-adverbiaux)), fused into a fixed idiomatic unit.

| Verb | Meaning | Example sentence |
|---|---|---|
| s'en aller | to go away, to leave | Il **s'en va** demain. (He's leaving tomorrow.) |
| s'en sortir | to manage, to cope | Elle **s'en sort** bien malgré les difficultés. (She's managing well despite the difficulties.) |
| s'y prendre | to go about doing something (well or badly) | Tu **t'y prends** mal. (You're going about it the wrong way.) |
| s'y faire | to get used to something | Je **m'y fais** petit à petit. (I'm getting used to it little by little.) |
| en vouloir à quelqu'un | to hold a grudge against someone | Elle **lui en veut**. (She holds it against him/her.) |
| s'en faire | to worry | Ne **t'en fais** pas. (Don't worry.) |

**Even here, there's no particle movement.** The *en*/*y* stays fixed to the verb; you can't split it off and relocate it the way English can with "turn the light **off**." The whole cluster — reflexive pronoun + verb + en/y — has to be learned and used as one rigid unit.

## Verbes à préposition fixe ("prepositional verbs")

A verb that always pairs with one specific preposition to reach its full meaning — structurally just an ordinary verb + preposition + object, with the preposition permanently glued to that verb's meaning.

| Verb | Meaning | Example sentence |
|---|---|---|
| compter sur | to count on | Je **compte sur** toi. (I'm counting on you.) |
| penser à | to think about | Elle **pense à** ses vacances. (She's thinking about her vacation.) |
| s'occuper de | to take care of | Il **s'occupe des** enfants. (He takes care of the children.) |
| dépendre de | to depend on | Ça **dépend de** la météo. (It depends on the weather.) |
| se souvenir de | to remember | Je **me souviens de** ce jour. (I remember that day.) |

**These map onto English's *inseparable* prepositional verbs** (look after, depend on, believe in) rather than true separable phrasal verbs (turn off, give up) — there's no equivalent of moving the preposition: you can never say *"je compte toi sur."*

### The rare exception: verbs that split between two prepositions

Almost every verb above is locked to one single preposition, with no alternative — that's the norm. A very small, closed set doesn't follow this, splitting its meaning across two different prepositions instead:

| Verb | With en | With à |
|---|---|---|
| croire | faith/trust — **croire en** Dieu, **croire en** toi (believe in God, believe in you) | accepting something exists/is valid — **croire à** la chance, **croire à** ce projet (believe in luck, believe this project is viable) |
| consister | **consister en** + noun — what something is made up of: *Le repas **consiste en** trois plats.* | **consister à** + infinitive — what something involves doing: *Le travail **consiste à** répondre aux emails.* |

**consister's split isn't quite the same phenomenon as croire's.** With *croire*, the same type of complement (a noun) takes a different preposition depending on the *meaning* — faith vs. belief-in-validity. With *consister*, the preposition instead tracks the *grammatical* shape of what follows: a noun pulls *en*, an infinitive pulls *à* — more mechanical than a free meaning choice. Either way, don't treat this pair as a hint that other verbs behave the same way — outside of *croire* and *consister*, this alternation isn't a pattern to expect.

## Locutions verbales (fixed verbal expressions)

Covered in detail in [Idioms](./05_idioms.md#locutions-figées-fixed-expressions) — the broader category of fixed multi-word phrases that happen to be built around a verb. A couple more, in the same spirit:

- **prendre son temps** (to take one's time) — Elle **prend son temps** pour tout. (She takes her time with everything.)
- **faire de son mieux** (to do one's best) — Il **a fait de son mieux**. (He did his best.)

## Points to note for French

### 1. There's no productive particle system to learn as a set

English phrasal verbs are learnable partly as a *system* — once you know that "up" often adds a sense of completion (eat up, finish up, use up), that pattern transfers across many verbs. **French idiomatic verbs don't work this way.** Each pronominal idiom or fixed-preposition verb has to be memorized individually as its own piece of vocabulary; there's no small set of French "particles" that predictably shift meaning across a wide range of verbs the way English particles do.

### 2. Nothing is separable

Every category above — pronominal idioms, fixed-preposition verbs, *locutions verbales* — keeps its pieces in a fixed order. Unlike English's separable phrasal verbs (where a pronoun object *forces* separation: "turn **it** off," never "turn off **it**"), French never splits these constructions apart at all.
