# Order of Adjectives in French (L'ordre des adjectifs)

English's [Royal Order](../../eng/intermediate/01_adj_royalorder.md) is a fairly rigid 9-category table that native speakers follow almost without thinking. **French doesn't have anything nearly that formalized** — and the reason traces straight back to something already covered in [Adjectives](../basic/10_adjectives.md#position-before-or-after-the-noun): most French adjectives sit **after** the noun by default, where a strict stacking order never had much reason to crystallize the way it did in English.

## Why French doesn't need as strict a system

- Only a small, closed set of adjectives (BAGS: beauty, age, goodness, size) ever sits *before* the noun — stacking more than one or two of them is already rare in natural speech, so there's little pressure to formalize their relative order.
- The much larger set of adjectives that sits *after* the noun (color, shape, origin, material, nationality, opinion-when-postposed) behaves more like English's loose **coordinate** category by default — joined with commas or *et*, reorderable, rather than locked into a fixed hierarchy.

## Stacking adjectives before the noun

When more than one BAGS-type adjective does pile up before a noun, there's a loose customary tendency — determiner/quantity, then ordinal (premier, dernier), then opinion/beauty, then size, then age, then the noun — but French grammar doesn't teach this as a memorized table the way English does.

- Sentence: les **trois premiers beaux** jours du printemps. (the first three beautiful days of spring)

Stacking three or more before a single noun already starts to sound stiff, the same practical ceiling English hits — in practice, French rarely goes past one or two.

## Ordering adjectives after the noun

Post-nominal adjectives lean on a much looser tendency: whatever sits **closest to the noun** tends to be the most intrinsic/classifying property (material, nationality, a fixed category), while more subjective or emphasis-driven adjectives sit further out or get chained on with *et*.

- Sentence: un **grand** chat **noir et blanc**. (a big black-and-white cat) — size (BAGS) stays before the noun; the two color adjectives, equal in weight, are joined by *et* after it — behaving exactly like English's coordinate adjectives.
- Sentence: une chemise **en coton bleu**. (a blue cotton shirt) — material (*coton*) sits closest to the noun-linking **en**, color (*bleu*) follows it, describing the material rather than the shirt directly.

There's no 9-slot table to memorize here — closeness to the noun tracks how intrinsic/classifying the property is, as a tendency, not a rule with a fixed number of categories.

## The real French equivalent: meaning changes with position

If there's one word-order rule about French adjectives worth learning as thoroughly as English's Royal Order, it isn't a stacking order at all — it's the [meaning-shift-by-position table already covered in Adjectives](../basic/10_adjectives.md#position-before-or-after-the-noun): *ancien*, *grand*, *pauvre*, *propre*, *cher*, *dernier*, *même*, *seul* all change meaning depending on which side of the noun they sit on. That's the genuinely load-bearing adjective-order fact in French — where English asks "what order do I stack these in," French more often asks "which side of the noun changes what this word means."
