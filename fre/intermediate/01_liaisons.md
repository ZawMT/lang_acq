# Liaisons: Compulsory, Prohibited, and Optional (Liaisons obligatoires, interdites et facultatives)

[Phonetics](../basic/02_phonetics.md#liaison) introduced the basic mechanic: a normally-silent final consonant gets pronounced when the next word starts with a vowel sound. What it didn't cover is that liaison isn't a single free-for-all rule — it splits into three categories, and native speakers apply them almost unconsciously.

## Liaisons obligatoires (compulsory)

These liaisons are never skipped, in any register.

| Context | Example | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| Determiner + noun | les**‿**amis, un**‿**ami, ces**‿**hommes | /le.z‿ami/, /œ̃.n‿ami/, /se.z‿ɔm/ |
| Subject pronoun + verb | nous**‿**avons, ils**‿**ont, on**‿**est | /nu.z‿avɔ̃/, /il.z‿ɔ̃/, /ɔ̃.n‿ɛ/ |
| Preceding adjective + noun | un grand**‿**homme, de beaux**‿**arbres | /œ̃.ɡʁɑ̃.t‿ɔm/, /də.bo.z‿aʁbʁ/ |
| Short adverb + adjective/adverb | très**‿**intéressant, trop**‿**heureux | /tʁɛ.z‿ɛ̃teʁesɑ̃/, /tʁo.p‿øʁø/ |
| A form of **est** (être) + what follows | il est**‿**ici, c'est**‿**évident | /il.ɛ.t‿isi/, /sɛ.t‿evidɑ̃/ |
| Number + noun | deux**‿**enfants, trois**‿**heures | /dø.z‿ɑ̃fɑ̃/, /tʁwa.z‿œʁ/ |
| Fixed expressions | de temps**‿**en temps, tout**‿**à fait | /də.tɑ̃.z‿ɑ̃tɑ̃/, /tu.t‿a.fɛ/ |

**One irregular pronunciation to flag: *neuf*.** Before **heures** and **ans**, the normally-silent-turned-/f/ liaison consonant actually voices to /v/: *neuf heures* is /nœ.v‿œʁ/, not /nœ.f‿œʁ/ — an exception inside an already-compulsory rule.

## Liaisons interdites (forbidden)

These liaisons never happen, even though the spelling might tempt you into making them.

| Context | Example (no liaison) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Before **h aspiré** | les # haricots, un # héros | the "aspirated h" blocks liaison even though it isn't pronounced (see [h muet vs. h aspiré](../basic/02_phonetics.md#consonant-sounds)) |
| After the conjunction **et** | un garçon et # une fille | *et* never liaises, full stop |
| Before **onze** and a few similar words | les # onze enfants | *onze* behaves as if it started with a consonant, historically |
| Singular noun + adjective or verb | un enfant # intelligent | contrast with a *pronoun* subject, where liaison to the same verb is compulsory: **il**_est arrivé vs. Jean # est arrivé |

That last row is the one worth sitting with: **the exact same verb form** takes liaison after a pronoun but not after a noun. *Il**‿**est arrivé* (compulsory) vs. *Jean # est arrivé* (forbidden) — the grammatical category of what precedes the verb, not just the sound, decides whether liaison happens.

## Liaisons facultatives (optional)

These can go either way — pronounced or not — and the choice is mostly about **register**, not meaning.

| Context | Example |
|---|---|
| Plural noun + adjective | des enfants**‿**intelligents *(or without liaison, in casual speech)* |
| Plural noun + verb | les enfants**‿**arrivent *(or without)* |
| Conjugated verb (not être) + what follows | il prend**‿**un livre *(or without)* |
| After **pas** | je ne suis pas**‿**arrivé *(or without)* |

**More liaisons get pronounced the more formal the setting.** A news broadcaster, a political speech, or a poem read aloud will use noticeably more optional liaisons than two friends chatting — this is a genuine sociolinguistic dial, not a right/wrong choice. Casual spoken French drops most optional liaisons; careful, formal, or read-aloud French keeps them.

## A liaison you should never make on purpose: la "faute de liaison"

Because liaison *feels* like "add a linking sound when in doubt," speakers sometimes insert one where none belongs — a well-known enough mistake that French even has slang names for the two ways it happens:

- **Un cuir** — wrongly inserting a /t/ sound: *"il va-t-à Paris"* (should be *il va à Paris*, no liaison consonant at all after *va*).
- **Un velours** — wrongly inserting a /z/ sound: *"moi-z-aussi"* (should be *moi aussi*).

These are genuine, recognized errors (sometimes made humorously on purpose), not a fourth liaison category — worth knowing about mainly so an occasional inserted sound in casual native speech doesn't get mistaken for a rule you missed.
