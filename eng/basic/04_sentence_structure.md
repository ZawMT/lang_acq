# Sentence Structure

A sentence is built from smaller units nested inside each other: words combine into phrases, phrases combine into clauses, and clauses combine into sentences.

## Word

The smallest meaningful unit. Every word plays one of the [parts of speech](./03_epos.md).

- Example: **dog**, **run**, **quickly**

## Phrase

A group of related words that acts as a single unit, but does **not** contain both a subject and a verb together.

| Type | Example | Acts like a |
|---|---|---|
| Noun phrase (NP) | **the big dog** | noun |
| Verb phrase (VP) | **has been running** | verb |
| Adjective phrase (AdjP) | **very happy** | adjective |
| Adverb phrase (AdvP) | **quite quickly** | adverb |
| Prepositional phrase (PP) | **in the morning** | adjective or adverb |

- Sentence: **The big dog** ran **in the morning**.

## Clause

A group of words that contains both a **subject** and a **predicate** (a verb doing something). A clause is one level up from a phrase.

- **Independent (main) clause** — can stand alone as a complete sentence.
  - Example: **She left early.**
- **Dependent (subordinate) clause** — cannot stand alone; it depends on a main clause.
  - Example: **because she was tired** (needs a main clause: "She left early **because she was tired**.")

## Predicate

The part of a clause that says something about the subject. It's everything in the clause except the subject, built around the main verb.

- Sentence: The dog **chased the ball in the park**.
  - Subject: The dog
  - Predicate: chased the ball in the park

**Careful with sentences that have more than one clause.** "Predicate" applies per clause, not just once for the whole sentence. Take: "She left early because she was tired."

Loosely, if you only look at the top-level subject ("She"), everything else — "left early because she was tired" — can be called the predicate of the sentence. But the sentence actually contains two clauses, each with its own subject and predicate, and the second clause is attached to the first as a modifier rather than being swallowed into its predicate:

| Clause | Subject | Predicate |
|---|---|---|
| She left early *(independent)* | She | left early |
| because she was tired *(dependent)* | she | was tired |

The dependent clause "because she was tired" answers *why* she left — it modifies the main clause the way an adverb would, rather than being part of the main clause's predicate.

## Sentence

One or more clauses expressing a complete thought, starting with a capital letter and ending with `.`, `!`, or `?`.

| Type | Made of | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | one independent clause | She left early. |
| Compound | two+ independent clauses joined by a conjunction | She left early, **and** he stayed late. |
| Complex | one independent + one or more dependent clauses | She left early **because she was tired**. |
| Compound-complex | two+ independent + one or more dependent clauses | She left early **because she was tired**, **and** he stayed late. |

### Sentence types by purpose

The table above classifies sentences by their **clause structure**. Sentences can also be classified by their **purpose** — what the sentence is trying to do:

| Type | Purpose | Example | End punctuation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Declarative | makes a statement | She left early. | `.` |
| Interrogative | asks a question | Did she leave early? | `?` |
| Imperative | gives a command or request | Leave early. | `.` or `!` |
| Exclamatory | expresses strong emotion | She left so early! | `!` |

These two classifications are independent — any purpose-type can also be simple, compound, complex, or compound-complex. For example, "Did she leave early because she was tired?" is both **interrogative** and **complex**.

"Affirmative" and "negative" aren't separate sentence types on their own — they describe whether a sentence (of any type above) confirms something or denies it.

- Affirmative: She left early.
- Negative: She **didn't** leave early.

## Expression

An informal, everyday term for any string of words that conveys a meaning — a word, a phrase, an idiom, or even a clause fragment. It isn't a strict grammatical category like phrase or clause; it's just a general way to talk about "a bit of language."

- Example: "**break the ice**" (an idiomatic expression, grammatically a verb phrase)

See [Idioms and Fixed Expressions](./05_idioms.md) for more on idioms, fixed expressions, and collocations.

## How they fit together

```
Word  →  Phrase  →  Clause (Subject + Predicate)  →  Sentence
```

- A **word** is the smallest piece.
- **Words** group into a **phrase** (no subject+verb pair of its own).
- A **subject** plus a **predicate** (which is itself often a verb phrase) forms a **clause**.
- One or more **clauses** form a **sentence**.
- **Expression** sits outside this ladder — it's a loose, informal label that can refer to a unit at any level.
