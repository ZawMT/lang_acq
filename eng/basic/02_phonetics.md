## Consonant clusters

A consonant cluster is two or more separate consonant **phonemes** pronounced together with no vowel between them, in the same syllable. Each sound keeps its own identity — you can pull the cluster apart and still hear the individual sounds.

### Clusters vs. affricates

It's easy to confuse a consonant cluster with an affricate (like `/tʃ/` or `/dʒ/` in the tables above), since both are written with two symbols. The difference is in how the sounds are produced:

- **Cluster** (e.g. `/kw/` in "queen"): two independent phonemes said in sequence. `/k/` finishes before `/w/` begins, and each could appear on its own elsewhere (e.g. `/k/` in "cat", `/w/` in "wet").
- **Affricate** (e.g. `/tʃ/` in "chip"): a single phoneme. It starts as a stop (`/t/`-like) and releases as a fricative (`/ʃ/`-like) in one uninterrupted articulation — native speakers hear it as one sound, not two, and it behaves as one sound in the syllable structure.

A practical test: a cluster can usually be split across two syllables in a related word (e.g. "cluster" itself splits as clus-ter), while an affricate cannot be split without turning into something else.

## Stress and intonation

Stress and intonation are about rhythm and pitch, not just individual sounds — they change which syllable or word stands out, and can even change meaning.

### Word stress

In any word with more than one syllable, one syllable is said more strongly than the others — louder, longer, and clearer. This is marked with a small `ˈ` mark just before the stressed syllable, and a `ˌ` mark before a lighter, secondary stress if there is one.

| Word | Syllables | Pronunciation | Stressed syllable |
|---|---|---|---|
| photograph | pho-to-graph | /ˈfəʊ-tə-ɡrɑːf/ | 1st |
| photography | pho-tog-ra-phy | /fə-ˈtɒɡ-rə-fi/ | 2nd |
| photographic | pho-to-graph-ic | /ˌfəʊ-tə-ˈɡræ-fɪk/ | 1st (secondary), 3rd (primary) |

Word stress can also be the only thing separating a noun from a verb that's spelled the same way:

| Word | As a noun | As a verb |
|---|---|---|
| record | ˈrecord /ˈrɛkɔːd/ | reˈcord /rɪˈkɔːd/ |
| present | ˈpresent /ˈprɛzənt/ | preˈsent /prɪˈzɛnt/ |
| object | ˈobject /ˈɒbdʒɪkt/ | obˈject /əbˈdʒɛkt/ |

### Sentence stress

In a sentence, **content words** (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs — the words that carry the meaning) are usually stressed, while **function words** (articles, prepositions, pronouns, auxiliary verbs — the grammatical "glue") are usually said quickly and weakly.

> **I** **WANT** to **BUY** a **NEW** **CAR**.

Here "I", "to", and "a" are function words and get little stress, while "WANT", "BUY", "NEW", and "CAR" carry the sentence's meaning and get the stress.

### Intonation

Intonation is the rise and fall of pitch across a phrase or sentence. English uses it to signal meaning beyond the words themselves:

- **Falling tune (↘)** — used for statements, commands, and wh- questions. It signals "this is complete/certain."
  - "I live in London. ↘"
  - "Where do you live? ↘"
- **Rising tune (↗)** — used for yes/no questions and to signal something is unfinished or uncertain.
  - "Do you live in London? ↗"
- **Fall-rise (↘↗)** — used to show politeness, doubt, or that more is coming.
  - "I liked it, ↘ but... ↗"
- **List intonation** — rising on each item except the last, which falls, to signal the list is complete.
  - "I bought apples ↗, oranges ↗, and bananas. ↘"
