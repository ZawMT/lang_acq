# Prepositional, Phrasal, and Phrasal-Prepositional Verbs

[Phrasal Verbs](../basic/19_phrasal_verbs.md) introduced the verb + particle pattern. In fact, English has three related patterns that are easy to mix up: **prepositional verbs** (verb + preposition), **phrasal verbs** (verb + adverb particle), and **phrasal-prepositional verbs** (verb + adverb + preposition). Telling them apart matters because they behave differently.

## Prepositional verbs (verb + preposition)

The preposition is fixed by the verb, is never optional, and never separates from its object. The meaning is usually still fairly close to the verb's normal meaning.

| Prepositional verb | Example sentence |
|---|---|
| depend on | It **depends on** the weather. |
| believe in | She **believes in** hard work. |
| listen to | **Listen to** me carefully. |
| look at | **Look at** this photo. |
| consist of | The team **consists of** five players. |
| insist on | He **insisted on** paying. |
| rely on | You can **rely on** her. |
| apologize for | I **apologized for** the delay. |
| look after | She **looks after** her little brother. |
| get over | He finally **got over** his cold. |

Test: unlike separable phrasal verbs, the object always goes after the preposition, even a pronoun — "**Listen to** me," never "*Listen me to*." In a question, the preposition can move to the front in formal style, or stay stranded at the end in everyday speech: "**To** whom are you listening?" / "Who are you **listening to**?"

### Same verb, different preposition, different meaning

A number of verbs change meaning depending on which preposition follows.

| Verb | With preposition A | With preposition B |
|---|---|---|
| agree | **agree with** a person | **agree on** a plan / **agree to** a proposal |
| apply | **apply for** a job | **apply to** a school/organization |
| care | **care for** = look after | **care about** = be concerned with |
| think | **think of** = recall/consider | **think about** = reflect on |

## Phrasal-prepositional verbs (verb + adverb + preposition)

These three-word combinations were mentioned briefly in [Phrasal Verbs](../basic/19_phrasal_verbs.md#three-word-phrasal-verbs-always-inseparable); here is a broader working list. They're always inseparable, and the object always comes at the very end.

| Phrasal-prepositional verb | Meaning | Example sentence |
|---|---|---|
| look forward to | anticipate with pleasure | I **look forward to** your reply. |
| put up with | tolerate | I can't **put up with** this noise. |
| get away with | avoid punishment for | He **got away with** cheating. |
| look down on | consider inferior | Don't **look down on** others. |
| come up with | think of, produce | She **came up with** a great idea. |
| cut down on | reduce | He's **cutting down on** sugar. |
| catch up on | do delayed work | I need to **catch up on** sleep. |
| run out of | have none left | We **ran out of** milk. |
| keep up with | stay at the same pace as | It's hard to **keep up with** the news. |
| do away with | eliminate | The rule was **done away with** years ago. |

## Telling phrasal verbs and prepositional verbs apart

Since both can look like "verb + short word," two quick tests help distinguish them.

| Test | Phrasal verb (verb + adverb particle) | Prepositional verb (verb + preposition) |
|---|---|---|
| Stress | particle is often stressed: "Turn it **OFF**." | preposition is unstressed: "**Look** at it." |
| Pronoun object placement | separable ones allow the pronoun before the particle: **turn it off** | pronoun always follows the preposition: **look at it** (never *look it at*) |

## When object position changes the meaning

For a few verb + preposition pairs, the exact same two words carry a different meaning depending on whether an object sits between them.

### "Run into" / "walk into": idiomatic meaning vs. literal collision

| Pattern | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Verb + Preposition + Object (nothing between verb and preposition) | I **ran into** an old friend at the mall. | met by chance |
| Verb + Object + Preposition + Object (an object comes between verb and preposition) | He **ran** the shopping cart **into** a display stand. | caused to crash/collide |
| Verb + Preposition + Object (nothing between verb and preposition) | He **walked into** their trap. | fell for a trick |
| Verb + Object + Preposition + Object (an object comes between verb and preposition) | She **walked** her dog **into** the vet's office. | led/brought by walking (literal) |

With nothing between the verb and the preposition, the preposition is glued to the verb as part of a fixed [prepositional verb](#prepositional-verbs-verb--preposition) with an idiomatic meaning ("encounter unexpectedly," "fall for a trick"). As soon as a direct object of the verb appears first, the preposition detaches and becomes an ordinary preposition of direction — the verb goes back to its literal meaning, and the prepositional phrase is just a separate piece describing where the object ended up.

This is a genuinely rare pattern — most verb + preposition combinations don't have both an idiomatic "no object in between" reading and a literal "object in between" reading. Don't confuse it with the far more common case of a phrasal verb that is simply **always inseparable**, with no alternate reading at all — see [Prepositional verbs](#prepositional-verbs-verb--preposition) above for a list (depend on, believe in, look after, get over, etc.). Those never take an object between verb and particle, in any meaning.

Importantly, this isn't a case of "moving the object" changing the meaning of one fixed phrasal verb — a true separable phrasal verb (like "put down" or "turn down" [above](#one-verb-several-particles-several-meanings)) never changes meaning when its object moves, by definition of being separable. What's really happening with "run into"/"walk into" is that the identical word sequence belongs to two entirely different grammatical structures: an idiomatic prepositional verb with no object of its own, versus an ordinary transitive verb followed by a separate prepositional phrase. Whether an object appears right after the verb tells you which structure you're in — nothing is actually being switched.

### "Run up": phrasal verb vs. literal direction

| Pattern | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Verb + Object (particle "up" attached to the verb) | He **ran up** a huge phone bill. | accumulated (idiomatic phrasal verb) |
| Verb + Preposition + Object | He **ran up** the stairs. | moved upward (literal direction) |

Both sentences look identical on the surface, but only the idiomatic one passes the separable-phrasal-verb test — "He ran a huge bill up" is unusual but understood as the same idiom, while "He ran the stairs up" is simply wrong, because in the literal sentence "up" is a preposition governing "the stairs," not a particle attached to "ran."

## One verb, several particles, several meanings

Many common verbs form a whole family of unrelated phrasal verbs depending on the particle attached.

| Base verb | Phrasal verb | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| call | call off | cancel |
| call | call on | ask/visit |
| call | call for | require |
| call | call up | telephone |
| put | put off | postpone |
| put | put on | wear |
| put | put away | store |
| put | put down | set down / euthanize (an animal) |
| put | put up with | tolerate |
| turn | turn down | reject (an offer) / lower (volume, heat) |

Note that "put down" and "turn down" are genuinely separable phrasal verbs, but — unlike "run into" or "run up" — separating them never changes their meaning. "Put the dog down" / "put down the dog" mean the same thing, whichever sense (literally setting it down, or euthanizing it) the context calls for; likewise "turn down the job offer" / "turn the job offer down" both mean reject, and "turn down the radio" / "turn the radio down" both mean lower the volume. Their multiple meanings come from the object and context, not from word order — that's true of nearly all separable phrasal verbs, which is exactly what makes "run into"/"run up" unusual.
