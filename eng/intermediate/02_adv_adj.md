# Adverbs vs. Adjectives

Adjectives and adverbs (see [Adjectives](../basic/10_adjectives.md) and [Adverbs](../basic/11_adverbs.md)) are easy to confuse because a handful of words look identical in both roles, and because some verbs take an adjective even though a verb "should" be modified by an adverb. This file covers those trouble spots, plus how adverbs are ordered and placed in a sentence.

## Flat adverbs (same form as the adjective)

Some adverbs don't add "-ly" at all — they're identical to the adjective form.

| Word | As adjective | As adverb |
|---|---|---|
| fast | a **fast** car | She runs **fast**. |
| hard | a **hard** exam | He works **hard**. |
| late | a **late** train | She arrived **late**. |
| early | an **early** meeting | We left **early**. |
| high | a **high** shelf | The bird flew **high**. |
| straight | a **straight** line | Go **straight** ahead. |
| wrong | the **wrong** answer | You guessed **wrong**. |

## Colloquial and poetic flat adverbs

Beyond the standard flat adverbs above, everyday speech, idioms, and song lyrics often stretch the same pattern to words that formal grammar would still expect "-ly" on. This is not standard written English, but it's common and usually deliberate.

| Informal / poetic | Standard "-ly" form | Where it shows up |
|---|---|---|
| **Love me true** | Love me truly | song lyric ("Love Me Tender") |
| The story **rings true** | *rings truly* (unused) | fixed idiom |
| **Drive safe** | Drive safely | spoken farewell |
| **Play nice** | Play nicely | said to children |
| **Hold tight** | Hold tightly | idiom |

Two different reasons explain this:

- **Idiom**: phrases like "ring true," "hold true," and "come true" have used the flat form of "true" for centuries — it's the established idiomatic form, not an error.
- **Rhythm and warmth**: in song, poetry, and casual speech, the "-ly" form adds a syllable and a more formal, distant tone. "Love me true" fits the melody's rhythm and feels more direct and intimate than "love me truly" would — the same trade-off behind "drive safe" sounding like a quick, warm send-off where "drive safely" sounds like an instruction.

## Confusable pairs: flat form vs. "-ly" form

For a few of these words, adding "-ly" doesn't just make an adverb — it changes the meaning entirely, giving two unrelated adverbs.

| Flat form | Meaning | "-ly" form | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| hard | with effort | hardly | barely, almost not |
| late | not on time | lately | recently |
| near | close by | nearly | almost |
| high | to a high point | highly | very, to a great degree |

- Sentence: He works **hard** every day, so he's **hardly** ever free.
- Sentence: I haven't seen her **lately**; she came home **late** last night.
- Sentence: The book is **highly** recommended, though the shelf itself is quite **high**.

## Good vs. well

"Good" is always an adjective. "Well" is normally its adverb, but "well" is also an adjective when it specifically means "in good health."

- Adverb: She sings **well**. (describes the verb "sings")
- Adjective (health): I don't feel **well** today.
- Adjective (quality): This is a **good** song. (not "a well song")

## Linking verbs take adjectives, not adverbs

A linking verb connects the subject to a description of the subject itself, not to an action — so it's followed by an adjective, not an adverb (see [Linking verbs](../basic/12_verbs_overview.md)).

| Linking verb | Example sentence |
|---|---|
| be | She **is happy**. |
| seem / appear | He **seems tired**. |
| become / get | It **got dark**. |
| feel | I **feel bad** about it. |
| look | She **looks angry**. |
| smell / taste / sound | The soup **tastes good**. |

Compare **feel bad** vs. **feel badly**: "I feel bad" describes the subject's emotional state (adjective). "I feel badly" literally claims your sense of touch is impaired — the adverb badly modifies how you feel with your hands, which is almost never the intended meaning.

## Adverb placement

Adverbs can go in three general positions, but frequency and degree adverbs (see [Adverbs](../basic/11_adverbs.md#by-function)) usually sit in **mid-position**: after "be," after the first auxiliary, or before the main verb.

| Position | Example |
|---|---|
| Front | **Yesterday**, she called me. |
| Mid | She **always** arrives early. She has **never** been to Paris. |
| End | She called me **yesterday**. |

## Order of multiple adverbs: Manner, Place, Time

When several adverbials stack up in one sentence, they follow a fixed order — Manner, then Place, then Time (a rough parallel to the fixed [adjective order](./01_adj_royalorder.md)).

- Sentence: She sang **beautifully** (manner) **at the concert** (place) **last night** (time).
- Sentence: He worked **quietly** (manner) **in the library** (place) **all afternoon** (time).
