# Order of Adjectives (Royal Order)

When more than one descriptive adjective (see [Adjectives](../basic/10_adjectives.md)) sits in front of a noun, native speakers put them in a fixed order almost without thinking. Getting this order wrong is one of the clearest signs of a non-native sentence, even when every individual adjective is correct.

## The order

| Order | Category | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Determiner / Quantity | a, the, two, several, my |
| 2 | Opinion | lovely, ugly, delicious, boring |
| 3 | Size | big, small, tiny, huge |
| 4 | Age | old, young, new, ancient |
| 5 | Shape | round, square, long |
| 6 | Color | red, blue, green |
| 7 | Origin | French, Burmese, American |
| 8 | Material | wooden, silk, plastic |
| 9 | Purpose | sleeping (bag), racing (car) |
| — | Noun | box, dress, car |

E.g. A beautiful big old round blue Burmese bamboo billiard table

- Sentence: She bought a **lovely old French** clock. (opinion → age → origin)
- Sentence: He wore a **small red cotton** jacket. (size → color → material)
- Sentence: They gave her a **beautiful little wooden** jewelry box. (opinion → size → material → purpose)

Stacking every category in one sentence is technically possible but sounds unnatural — "two lovely small old round red French wooden sleeping bags" is grammatical but nobody talks like that. In practice, speakers rarely combine more than two or three adjectives before a noun.

## Coordinate vs. cumulative adjectives

Not every string of adjectives follows the fixed order above — it depends on whether the adjectives are **coordinate** (equal, same category) or **cumulative** (building on each other, different categories).

| Type | Rule | Test | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coordinate | same category, equal weight | can reorder; can insert "and" | a **big, heavy** box → a **heavy, big** box → a **big and heavy** box |
| Cumulative | different categories, fixed order | cannot reorder; "and" sounds wrong | a **big red** box → *a red big box* (wrong) / *a big and red box* (odd) |

- Coordinate adjectives are separated by a **comma** (or "and"): a **tall, confident** speaker.
- Cumulative adjectives take **no comma**, since they follow the Royal Order table above: a **tall young** speaker.

The reorder/"and" test is the real authority here — the Royal Order table only predicts the outcome reliably for objective, classifying properties of things (age, shape, color, origin, material, purpose). It's less reliable once an opinion word is paired with a physical trait describing a *person*, since those often read as independent, parallel observations rather than a hierarchy. "Confident" outranks "tall" in the table, but "a tall, confident speaker" and "a confident, tall speaker" are both natural — the pair behaves as coordinate, not cumulative, so you're not forced into the table's order or into dropping the comma.
