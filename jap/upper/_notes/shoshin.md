# Shoshin: 初心忘るべからず (Zeami)

**初心忘（しょしんわす）るべからず** (shoshin wasuru bekarazu) = "Never forget shoshin."

One of the best-known sayings in Japanese, from **世阿弥（ぜあみ）** (Zeami Motokiyo, c. 1363–1443), the actor and playwright who shaped **能（のう）** (Noh theatre).

## Source

The phrase comes from **花鏡（かきょう）** (Kakyō, *The Mirror of the Flower*, 1424), Zeami's treatise on the art of Noh. His more famous treatise is **風姿花伝（ふうしかでん）** (Fūshi Kaden), the source of 秘（ひ）すれば花（はな） ("What is hidden is the flower").

The original passage, in classical Japanese:

> 当流（とうりゅう）に万能一徳（ばんのういっとく）の一句（いっく）あり。初心忘るべからず。此（この）句、三ヶ条（さんかじょう）の口伝（くでん）あり。
> 是非（ぜひ）とも初心忘るべからず。
> 時々（じじ）の初心忘るべからず。
> 老後（ろうご）の初心忘るべからず。

> "Our school has one phrase that is good for everything: never forget shoshin. It is passed down orally in three parts: never forget the shoshin of the beginning; never forget the shoshin of each stage; never forget the shoshin of old age."

## Zeami's meaning

For Zeami, **初心** is **your inexperienced, clumsy self when you started**: the awkward, unskilled level of a beginner.

Remembering it:

- keeps you **humble**: you don't become arrogant about your skill
- shows how far you've come, so you can judge your **real level**
- keeps you **growing**: you don't fall back into old faults

He also warns: 初心を忘るれば初心へかへる理（ことわり）. If you forget your beginner's self, you **return** to it. Forgetting where you started makes you slip back.

## The three shoshin

| Term | Stage | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **是非（ぜひ）の初心** | the beginning | when you first take up the art; remember those early mistakes |
| **時々（じじ）の初心** | each stage | every new level of a career is a beginning, with its own unfamiliar challenges |
| **老後（ろうご）の初心** | old age | even a master meets things for the first time as they age; learning never ends |

So shoshin isn't left behind once. You become a beginner **again** at every stage of life.

## Modern usage

Today 初心忘るべからず usually means "**don't forget your original resolve or enthusiasm**." It's a favourite in:

- welcome speeches to new employees (入社式（にゅうしゃしき）)
- graduation and school-entrance speeches
- wedding speeches: don't forget how you felt at the start

This is a later shift: Zeami stressed remembering your **immaturity**; modern use stresses your **first passion**. Both are common, so read the context.

## Zen "beginner's mind"

In English, "shoshin" is best known from **Zen**, through Shunryu Suzuki's *Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind* (1970):

> "In the beginner's mind there are many possibilities, but in the expert's there are few."

| | Zeami | Zen (Suzuki) |
|---|---|---|
| tradition | Noh theatre, the art of performance | Zen Buddhism |
| focus | remember your past immaturity | stay open, free of assumptions |
| goal | humility and lifelong growth | fresh, open perception |

Related ideas, but from different traditions.

## Grammar: 忘るべからず

Classical Japanese (文語（ぶんご）):

| Part | Meaning | Modern equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| **忘る** | to forget (classical form) | 忘れる |
| **べし** | should, must | べきだ |
| **べからず** | must not (negative of べし) | 〜てはいけない, 〜するな |

So 忘るべからず ≈ 忘れてはいけない (you must not forget).

**べからず** still appears on old-style signs and set phrases:

- 立入（たちい）るべからず (No entry)
- 働（はたら）かざる者（もの）食（く）うべからず (He who does not work shall not eat)
